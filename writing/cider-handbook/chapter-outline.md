# Chapter outline

Twenty short chapters; approximately 900–1,200 French words each. Vocabulary below is a seed list, not a required glossary or a quota. Reuse established words in later contexts. Every practical claim must pass the research guide; the dependencies here identify especially important checks.

## 1. Une pomme, beaucoup de possibilités — One Apple, Many Possibilities

Open with the difference between seeing a drink and seeing the decisions behind it. Introduce the tree-to-glass journey, cider's broad diversity and the continuing dry, still example. Explain how to read the guide without owning equipment.

Outcome: describe the main stages and distinguish juice from cider.
Language: present-tense explanation and sequence; *le jus, le cidre, une étape, transformer, obtenir*.
Research: basic fermentation explanation and any claims about cider definitions. Leave detailed instructions to later chapters.

## 2. Apprendre à goûter une pomme — Learning to Taste an Apple

Compare sweetness, acidity, bitterness, astringency and aroma through concrete sensations. A good eating apple prompts a question about a good blend.

Outcome: describe fruit without calling everything simply sweet or sour.
Language: comparisons, *plus… que*, *moins… que*, *tandis que*; *sucré, acide, amer, les tanins, une sensation*.
Research: sensory distinctions; apple categories and regional classification differences. Revisit in chapters 9, 16 and 19.

## 3. Choisir son pommier — Choosing an Apple Tree

Introduce site, climate, variety, rootstock, tree size and pollination. Include a reader with a small garden and a reader buying fruit from a grower.

Outcome: know which questions to ask before choosing a tree or supplier.
Language: purpose and suitability, *pour, afin de, convenir à*; *un verger, une variété, un porte-greffe, fleurir*.
Research: local suitability, compatibility and named cultivars. No universal planting recommendation.

## 4. Une année au verger — A Year in the Orchard

Follow seasonal observation, pruning aims, blossom, fruit set and basic care. Explain what to observe before intervening; keep specialist disease diagnosis outside scope.

Outcome: connect orchard decisions and seasonal conditions with the eventual crop.
Language: time and conditions, *lorsque, avant de, si*; *un bourgeon, une branche, tailler, la floraison*.
Research: seasonal timing, pruning limits, soil and water guidance, crop variability.

## 5. Le bon moment pour récolter — The Right Moment to Harvest

Explore ripeness, sampling, harvest choices, sorting and storage. Distinguish different varieties and avoid a single visual ripeness rule.

Outcome: explain how fruit is selected and why damaged or unsuitable fruit is excluded.
Language: evidence and uncertainty, *sembler, vérifier, ne… pas forcément*; *mûr, récolter, trier, conserver*.
Research: maturity indicators, fallen fruit, spoilage and storage conditions.

## 6. Un petit atelier bien préparé — Preparing a Small Workspace

Walk through equipment by function: preparation, pressing, fermentation, measurement and transfer. Explain bought-juice access, suitable materials, cleaning and sanitation as separate actions.

Outcome: understand what each item does and how to prepare for work.
Language: instructions and necessity, *il faut, avoir besoin de, rincer*; *un récipient, nettoyer, désinfecter, un tuyau*.
Research: equipment suitability, products and label-specific sanitation instructions, purchased juice selection.

## 7. Broyer et presser — Milling and Pressing

Follow fruit through preparation, milling and pressing with attention to texture, mess, effort and yield variation. Equipment differences matter more than a heroic effort with the press.

Outcome: explain the purpose and order of the operations.
Language: sequence and passive forms in moderation; *broyer, le marc, un pressoir, extraire*.
Research: safe equipment use, preparation practices, extraction and oxidation. Any yield figures need conditions.

## 8. Lire son jus — Understanding Your Juice

Introduce sampling, density, temperature effects, acidity and pH as different kinds of information. Use words and one carefully verified numerical example at a time.

Outcome: understand what an instrument can and cannot tell you.
Language: quantities and limits, *indiquer, mesurer, environ, cela ne signifie pas que*; *la densité, un échantillon, un densimètre*.
Research: measurement methods, calibration, correction and interpretation. No claim that pH equals total acidity or density equals sugar alone in all contexts.

## 9. Choisir un équilibre — Choosing a Balance

Return to the apples as a prospective blend. Discuss small trials, recording proportions and choosing a realistic intended style before making adjustments.

Outcome: connect fruit character with a reasoned choice rather than a universal recipe.
Language: preferences and tradeoffs, *préférer, plutôt que, en revanche*; *assembler, une proportion, équilibré*.
Research: pre-fermentation blending and adjustment boundaries. Distinguish this from finished-cider blending in chapter 16.

## 10. Les levures entrent en scène — Enter the Yeast

Explain the agents of fermentation, selected versus spontaneous fermentation, temperature and nutrition. Establish the researched route for the continuing example.

Outcome: understand what yeast needs and why methods differ.
Language: cause and consequence, *parce que, grâce à, donc*; *une levure, fermenter, ensemencer*.
Research: yeast-specific directions, nutrient decisions and spontaneous-fermentation variability. Personification may introduce, never replace, mechanism.

## 11. Suivre la fermentation — Following Fermentation

Watch the example through changing activity, measurements, aroma and temperature. Explain why bubbles alone cannot establish progress or completion.

Outcome: describe a monitoring routine and record meaningful observations.
Language: change over time, *devenir, diminuer, continuer à*; *un barboteur, dégager, ralentir*.
Research: venting, temperature, sampling and interpretation. Elapsed time is not the sole decision criterion.

## 12. Quand le cidre vous pose une question — When Cider Raises a Question

Organise common problems through short situations: little visible activity, an unexpected smell, an uncertain measurement. Separate observation, possible explanation and justified next action.

Outcome: gather information before attempting a remedy.
Language: hypotheses, *peut-être, il se peut que, si…*; *une odeur, vérifier, suspecter, reprendre*.
Research: faults, stalled fermentation and when to seek specialist advice or discard. Never diagnose safety by taste or smell.

## 13. Soutirer sans précipitation — Racking Without Rushing

Explain sediment, transfer and oxygen exposure, including reasons to transfer or leave the cider alone. Follow the example's changing volume.

Outcome: understand the purpose and tradeoffs of racking.
Language: avoiding an outcome, *éviter de, sans, risquer de*; *les lies, soutirer, un dépôt, transférer*.
Research: timing, vessel filling, contact materials and oxygen management.

## 14. Laisser le temps travailler — Giving It Time

Explore maturation, clarity, changing flavour, storage and repeated observations. Distinguish a visual preference from readiness or stability.

Outcome: explain what to monitor during maturation.
Language: duration and comparison over time; *s'affiner, limpide, trouble, évoluer*.
Research: maturation conditions, clarification choices and limitations of appearance.

## 15. Sec, doux, tranquille ou pétillant ? — Dry, Sweet, Still or Sparkling?

Explain these as separate dimensions. Keep the continuing batch dry and still; discuss other routes and their prerequisites without presenting them as casual finishing shortcuts.

Outcome: distinguish desired sweetness, carbonation and microbiological stability.
Language: contrast and conditional advice; *sec, doux, tranquille, pétillant, une reprise de fermentation*.
Research: refermentation, stabilization, carbonation and pressure. Advanced methods require reliable complete procedures before any actionable detail.

## 16. L'art des petits assemblages — The Art of Small Blends

Return to sensory balance with measured trials of finished cider. State explicitly when a hypothetical second batch is introduced; do not conjure new ingredients in the continuing example.

Outcome: compare trials and understand scaling and stability implications.
Language: proportion and evaluation; *un essai, mélanger, une note, dominer*.
Research: blending arithmetic and consequences of residual fermentable sugar. No tasting preference overrides packaging requirements.

## 17. Mettre en bouteille au bon moment — Bottling at the Right Time

Bring readiness, sanitation, packaging and closure together for the dry, still example. Explain what still needs checking even when fermentation appears quiet.

Outcome: understand the conditions required before packaging.
Language: prerequisites, *avant de, tant que, seulement si*; *embouteiller, une fermeture, la pression*.
Research: verified completion and stability criteria, appropriate containers, oxygen and closure practices. No universal priming dose or improvised bottle-heating procedure.

## 18. Conserver et servir — Storing and Serving

Follow the bottles into storage and eventually to the table. Include opening, sediment, glassware and serving temperature as contextual choices.

Outcome: describe storage and service without rigid tasting rituals.
Language: practical suggestions and degree; *frais, verser, conserver, délicatement*.
Research: storage conditions, packaging limits and supported shelf-life statements.

## 19. Mettre des mots sur un verre — Finding Words for a Glass

Build a clear tasting description from appearance, aroma, mouthfeel, balance and finish. Explain food pairings as comparisons the reader can explore.

Outcome: express a preference and support it with specific observations.
Language: *rappeler, évoquer, persister, s'accorder avec*; recycle chapter 2's distinctions.
Research: flavour claims and cultural examples. Metaphors describe perception; they do not prove ingredients or processes.

## 20. La prochaine récolte — The Next Harvest

Revisit the example and the orchard, showing how useful notes turn experience into better questions. Summarise the route naturally and suggest changing one meaningful variable at a time.

Outcome: recount the process and explain a next experiment in French.
Language: passé composé for what was done, imparfait for background, future and conditional for plans.
Research: audit the entire example and all numerical continuity. Close with curiosity, not a claim that the reader has mastered every method.

## Progression checks

Chapters 1–5 establish senses, fruit and description; 6–9 establish actions and measurement; 10–14 develop explanation, observation and uncertainty; 15–18 develop conditions and decisions; 19–20 consolidate description and reflection.

These are opportunities for useful French, not compulsory grammar lessons. Do not force a tense or construction into an unnatural sentence. Each chapter should briefly reactivate an earlier distinction and finish with a natural connection to the next topic.
