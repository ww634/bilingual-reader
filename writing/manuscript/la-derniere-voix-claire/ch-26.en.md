# What He Could Give Back

The visit took place two days later. Helen had begun the arrangements after their first conversation. Michael had agreed to meet Daniel; after receiving his letter, he had confirmed that he still wanted to. The appointment and Daniel's attendance had been authorised. Helen had warned him not to bring anything that had not been agreed.

He had left his telephone and his bag as instructed. Without them, he no longer knew what to do with his right hand. In the room where they were waiting, the file was in front of Helen. Daniel had nothing to arrange in the right order.

Michael came in with the slowness of a man protecting a knee. He had a grey beard, short, and a small cut on his chin. Daniel had imagined his face aged from a press photograph. He had not thought about the morning shave.

Michael greeted Helen, then looked at Daniel.

“You've changed.”

“Yes.”

“So have I. Sit down.”

Helen asked whether he wanted to begin with the letter. Michael said yes, without taking his eyes off Daniel.

“I attributed to you words I could not identify with that degree of certainty,” Daniel said.

“With what degree of certainty can you do it today?”

“I cannot maintain a reliable identification of the first name.”

“Then start with that.”

Daniel started again. He explained what he had done at Ruth's house, the suggested name, the repeated playbacks, the agreement he had subsequently treated as confirmation. He did not speak of good intentions.

“Is that written down, all of it?”

Helen indicated the letter and its supplement. Her client asked her to keep them together.

“Can you take it back again?” Michael asked.

Daniel looked at the pages.

“If something new changes my analysis, I'll have to explain it. I won't be able to make disappear what I've written here.”

Michael made a small gesture to Helen.

“A copy for me,” he said. “With all the pages.”

They spoke of what would change. Helen needed to compare these observations with the material presented at trial and the independent opinion. Michael knew the stages. He corrected a date in the summary of his previous application. Daniel had old files. Michael had lived between their dates.

“I cannot promise you release,” Daniel said.

“You're not the one with the keys. I understand.”

Michael put both hands on the table.

“Do you want to ask me why I lied?”

Daniel said that Helen had read him his statement.

“I'd asked for money. I'd said I wouldn't need her. A few weeks later, I was outside her office.”

The cut on his chin stretched as he tightened his jaw.

“She said no. I hit the door. I wanted her to open it. To go on answering me.”

Daniel did not write in his head a softer word in its place.

“When they asked me whether I'd been there, I said no. I went on. I thought admitting the lie would make me everything else.”

“And that evening?” Daniel asked.

“At home. Alone. That's still the same answer. I haven't got a better one for your visit.”

Helen recalled what the file allowed them to check. Nothing he had just said gave him a witness for the evening.

Daniel spoke about Claire, with the permission she had given him to pass on her account. He described the photograph, the two plates, the cake that was sliding.

Michael looked at Helen.

“Could I have a copy?”

She said she would ask. He brought his left hand back towards him.

“She used to put plants in my old shoes. Work boots. I'd told her to throw them away.”

Daniel waited for the rest.

“I'd wanted to take them when I left. She asked me whether I wanted the shoes or to leave her empty pots.”

He ran a finger over the table.

“I left them. It's not much of a story.”

Daniel could see the soil at the tops of the boots, the laces now useless. He did not know which plants Anna had put in them. He asked.

“Little white flowers. I don't know the name. She knew it.”

Towards the end, Michael asked what Daniel would do next. He told him about the work with Nisha, the hypotheses to test, the full report.

“I'm sorry,” Daniel said.

Michael nodded.

“You can be. Do the work anyway.”

As he left, Daniel waited for someone to open a door, then another. Outside, he found his telephone, his keys, the time at which he had planned to be home. He sent Helen the question to pass on to Claire about the photograph. Then he stayed for a while beside his car. He could leave.
