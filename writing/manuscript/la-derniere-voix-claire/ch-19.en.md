# Before She Agreed

Nisha called the following afternoon. Daniel had set aside the whole morning for a client who had stayed twenty minutes. For the rest of the time, he had checked that his phone was working. He let it ring twice before answering.

“I've recorded my first listening,” Nisha said. “Before opening your transcript.” “And?” “I can't identify that first name reliably.” Daniel tightened his fingers around his pen. The answer suited him. It hurt him. “Even in the processed version?” “It's more comfortable to listen to. That's not enough.”

She did not have a new sentence to offer. She had noted possible segments, hesitations and places where she could not decide. After reading the old proposed wording, she could understand how someone might hear that name. “I'm not telling you it's impossible. I'm telling you the signal doesn't justify your certainty.” Daniel wrote “preliminary assessment” above his notes. Nisha still had to finish her checks and her report.

Daniel asked what she had heard in the exchange at the beginning. Nisha had noted the question about two and Anna's reply. She had not identified the man. “It is not a speaker comparison,” she said. “You asked me to examine the words.” “I know.” Yet he had been waiting for a name.

Nisha asked him whether he had kept the abandoned trials. He gave her their reference numbers. “The one that removes too much speech is in the same folder.” “Good.” That word relieved him more than it should have. He had recommended Nisha to a firm of solicitors a few years earlier. He remembered emphasising her rigour. He had not added that he hoped to be exempt from it.

She had also listened to the copy of Ruth's cassette, at a later stage. “Have you gone back to the passage after the playback?” “The one where she speaks over it?” “After that. When the other machine stops.” Daniel opened his working project. Nisha gave him time references, without dictating a sentence. They agreed to speak again.

The hum formed persistent lines in the frequency display. Daniel selected a short section without voices to examine them. He tried narrow filters, restricted to those regions. Widening the notches also thinned the voices. He returned to the least intrusive setting. The background was still there. It took up less space.

Through headphones, in the quiet workshop, Daniel listened to a longer section. The overlap remained confused. He let the cursor move on. A click interrupted the voice coming from the second machine. Then his own voice filled the silence. “The first name, it's Michael. Listen again.”

Ruth repeated: “Michael… yes.” Another playback filled the room. After it stopped, Ruth's voice returned. “But I wouldn't have said it on my own.” Graham replied: “You recognise it now. That's what matters.” Then Daniel: “With the cleaning, it's clearer.”

He took off the headphones. The room seemed to him larger, as it might after several visitors had left. He remembered wanting to reassure Ruth. He had retained that memory with great care. The sentence about the cleaning had not been part of it. Now that he heard it, it did not seem unfamiliar.

He replayed the same section without filters. Some words remained difficult. The processing had allowed him to distinguish them better, but he now expected them. He marked the uncertain passages, then sent Nisha his settings and proposed wording. She had already recorded her own reading. Their records agreed on that exchange, without resolving the overlapping speech.

On the table, he laid out three pages. The letter of 22 October 2001. A visit record for the twenty-sixth, supplied with the case documents, listing Ruth, Graham and himself. Then the statement signed on the twenty-ninth. In the first, Ruth rejected the conversion of her doubt into an identification. In the last, she said she had recognised Michael.

The record did not date the magnetic tape. But the names, the home visit and the recorded playback session matched. The extract played in the sitting room followed the same sequence as the old working extract. Daniel noted those correspondences without turning them into a clock.

The statement of the twenty-ninth bore several signatures. Daniel stopped at Ruth's. He had thought of a woman who was finally certain. He now saw a document created after a conversation whose content he understood better. The signature did not say how that certainty had arisen. He photographed the three pages together for his record. Then he separated them. Putting them in order did not erase the days between them.

During their second call, Nisha let him explain the sequence of the documents. “She ends up hearing the same thing,” Daniel said. “After you give her the name.” “Yes.” “So her agreement does not constitute an independent check on your reading.” He looked at the three sheets. Ruth's agreement could no longer confirm his reading in the way he had imagined.

At five, Ben wrote to him to ask how the examination was going. Daniel began “There's progress”, then deleted the sentence. He called. “We can understand more of part of your mother's cassette. I gave her the name before she repeated it.” Ben said nothing for a few seconds. “I want to listen. The whole passage.” “Yes.” Daniel reopened the complete file.
