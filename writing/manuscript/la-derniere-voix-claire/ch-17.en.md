# An Ordinary Evening

The next day, Ben sent a message at three o'clock. “I'm back. Are you working?” Daniel was working and had been since eight. He had finished the transfer record, handed the cassette to the designated officer and sent the authorised documents. Then he had returned to Mabel Turner's interview. He replied: “Not for you.” The phone vibrated almost immediately. “So I can come round.”

Ben arrived half an hour later, with two bags. He put the smaller one on the workbench. “Biscuits. Elsie.” In the other, there were potatoes, eggs and bread. “Have you got a frying pan?” “I even have a kitchen.” “I saw the tomato. I'd rather ask.”

Daniel waited for him to talk about his mother. Ben took off his coat and asked where to throw away the receipt. Then he sat down on the stool. “She gave me three pairs of socks. She'd bought them before she left.” “Do they fit?” “Yes. It's annoying.” He looked at the silent speakers. “We talked. Not about everything. I told her I'd go back.” Daniel nodded. He left the notebook closed.

Ben noticed the chair against the wall. “Can I look at the leg?” “Not today.” “All right.” He turned the stool towards the screen. “The lady from the factory?” Daniel played the end of the authorised extract. The association had agreed to keep the story about the foreman. Mabel was laughing before she had finished her sentence.

“You left the cup in,” Ben said. “It hadn't done anything wrong.” A silence followed the laughter. Daniel stopped the cursor before the next sentence. “There?” Ben listened a second time. “A little later. She's catching her breath.” Daniel moved the end of the edit. He did not say that it was right. He kept the new position.

They began preparing the meal before five. Daniel had not had lunch. Ben claimed biscuits did not count. In the kitchen, the radio occupied half the worktop. Ben placed the potatoes in front of it. “Does it need all that space?” “It receives a lot of stations.”

Daniel found a saucepan while Ben washed the potatoes. They argued about their size. To Daniel, the pieces were too big. To Ben, they were not intended to be measured. Daniel cut one again. Ben looked at him, then passed him the rest of the chopping board. “Your project now.”

Daniel laughed before he could stop himself. “Marianne used to do that.” Ben dried his hands on a tea towel. “The potatoes?” “Let me finish whatever I'd started criticising.” He gathered the pieces with the knife. “Once, I explained that her route was wrong.” “Mistake.” “I was talking about the route.” “So was I.”

They had been going to a wedding in Yorkshire, Daniel explained. Marianne had planned a detour to avoid roadworks. He had taken over the map. Forty minutes later, they had found themselves in front of a closed gate. Beyond it, there were sheep. Marianne had asked which one was the groom.

“Were you late?” “For the ceremony. Not for the meal.” “Did she forgive you?” Daniel tipped the potatoes into the water. “She sent a Christmas card with a sheep on it to everyone. For three years.” Ben burst out laughing. Daniel remembered the cards lined up on that table, and Marianne discovering that she had bought the wrong envelopes. They had folded them. The sheep had a crease across the middle of their bodies. He told him that too.

Ben drained the potatoes, then browned them in the pan. He cracked the eggs over them while Daniel looked for the plates.

To lay the table, Daniel had to move a stack of magazines. The last one was open at a page he no longer recognised. He must have intended to read it. Ben waited with two glasses, without asking where to put the stack. Daniel put it on the sideboard. The table had a paler mark where the space had been cleared.

Ben brought over the pan. “Shall we help ourselves?” Daniel opened a drawer to find a trivet. There were batteries, string and four birthday candles. The trivet was in the next drawer. Ben put down the pan without comment. They ate the first mouthfuls in silence. The potatoes were a little overcooked. Daniel said nothing. Ben passed him the salt, then asked whether there was any bread left. There was.

Ben's phone vibrated during the meal. He read the message, then put the phone down. “A client?” “Not any more.” He had postponed an appointment twice. The owner had found someone else. Ben understood. He had still bought some of the materials. “Can you return it?” “Some of it. If I can find the invoices.”

Daniel began a sentence about organisation. His gaze fell on three envelopes wedged under the radio. He changed sentences. “I have a scanner. And an hour.” Ben looked at the envelopes in turn. “Shall we do yours too?” “Don't push it.”

After the meal, Ben took out a folder of receipts from his car. He selected the ones he needed. Daniel scanned them, then sent them to Ben without keeping a copy. Meanwhile, Ben wrote his return request. The money had not come back. Neither had the client. But the request had been sent.

At twenty to seven, Nisha Rao called. Daniel had written to her the previous day, specifying his own role in the case. Helen Ward had confirmed the terms of the examination. The authorised files and their documentation would be sent that evening. “I can start tomorrow,” Nisha said. “I'm not promising a conclusion tomorrow.” “All right.” “And not your transcript with the first delivery. I want to note what I hear before reading your proposed wording.” Daniel confirmed that they had arranged separate deliveries.

When he hung up, Ben was putting away the last receipts. “Tomorrow?” “She starts tomorrow.” Ben closed his folder. “I can come round after my job.” Daniel looked at the two plates still on the table. “Around six. I'll make something.” “No tomato.” Daniel opened his diary and wrote down the time.
