# Behind Her Voice

The chair stayed in the middle of the workshop after Shah left. Daniel walked around it twice before putting it away. He could work on the message. He knew how to begin. It was a relief that made him a little ashamed.

He set a microphone on a small stand, in front of the answering machine's speaker. The machine offered no audio output that he could use directly. He was not going to open it to save an hour. What he was preparing would be an acoustic copy: the sound of the machine, recorded in this room. It would have the faults of the message, plus those of the speaker and of the new recording.

On a sheet of paper, he wrote down the model of the answering machine, the date and the time. He photographed the display and the position of the microphone. Then he closed the courtyard door and stopped the small fan beside the computer. The resulting silence was not perfect. It was simply less bad.

The first playback served to set the recording level. On the screen, Daniel watched the peaks, those spikes that rose with the voice. He reduced the input gain to leave some headroom. A file that was too quiet could be turned up later. A clipped signal would not recover its lost detail because someone turned down the volume.

He started the recording before touching the answering machine. He kept the beginning, the breathing, the whole message and the time announcement. During playback, he did not move. At the end, he waited a few seconds before stopping.

The file received a plain name, with the date and the words “acoustic recording”. He saved it without processing, then made a working copy. It was still not the original file. The label could be impeccable; it repaired nothing.

He sent the copy and his notes by the method agreed with Shah. Then, he wrote at the top of a new page: “Where could she have been?” Not: “What did she know?” The message would not answer that second question.

Through headphones, Ruth seemed closer. That was an effect of listening, not information about her. Daniel listened to the sentences he already knew once through. He was listening for what happened between them.

After Anna's name, a distant voice spoke several syllables. The ending could have been “Mereford”. Before that name, he heard something that sounded like “bound for”. He wrote down the words in brackets. He was not certain of the beginning.

A noise rose over the announcement, like a wave erasing a line in the sand. On the waveform, it made a bulge. The bulge did not say “bus”. It showed only a change in the recorded signal. Daniel enlarged the passage on the screen so he could find it easily, not to see in it what he wanted to hear.

He tried reducing the low frequencies. The rumble became less intrusive. The announcement remained incomplete. He pushed the setting a little further. Ruth's voice lost some of its body; the distant voice was reduced to dry fragments. He undid that last change. Less noise. No more words.

He opened a display where time ran from left to right. Low frequencies were at the bottom, high frequencies at the top. The intensity appeared in colours. It was a spectrogram, another way of looking at the same signal. He could use it to locate the start of the rumble and the moment when the voices overlapped. He could not extract from it a word that had not been recorded.

He placed a marker before the announcement and another after Ruth's voice returned. The selection kept some context on either side. Isolating a syllable could change the way one heard it. He knew that, and yet his hand had already begun to narrow the selection. He moved back the first marker.

To compare, he alternated the unprocessed copy and the filtered version, at comparable volumes. He noted down the setting used and what it changed. By the third comparison, he was already anticipating the next syllable. He stopped playback instead of starting again. For five minutes, he sorted the cables in a drawer. He found one whose purpose he no longer knew. He kept it. That decision required no analysis.

He could have tried other tools. He owned enough to spend the night on it. But his question was not about the last word of the announcement. He restored the unprocessed version and listened to the noise itself.

The sound swelled, stayed for a moment, then receded. A passing vehicle was one possible explanation. A train, a coach, a lorry. Or a change of position of the receiver near a continuous noise. He had only a telephone message, followed by his own copy, to distinguish between them.

At seven, there was a knock at the door. The police officer who had come to collect the answering machine checked with him which message to preserve. Daniel gave him the machine and its power supply, with the notes he had prepared. He kept the handover document. Where the answering machine had been, the wood was lighter.

In the kitchen, he ate an apple and a slice of bread. He started a message to Judith. “If you need anything…” He deleted the sentence. After Marianne's death, he had received many offers of that kind. Working out what to ask for had been an extra task. He wrote that he could bring her groceries the next day. She replied that her sister was there, then thanked him.

When he put the headphones back on, he first listened to the seconds of silence after the message. A brief noise recurred at the end. He had heard it without thinking about it. It was his own chair, at the moment when he had stood up to stop the recording. He noted it in his notes. He had just added a noise to a story that already contained too many.

The rumble, however, was indeed present in the answering machine's playback. He had heard it from the very first time, before getting the microphone out. Ruth paused while it passed. That did not prove she was waiting for it, but it made that reading reasonable.

He opened a map of Calderwick. Mereford was to the north. Trains and buses went there. The announcement did not even imply that Ruth wanted to go there. She could have been waiting for someone beside a departure point.

The modern station stood a twenty-minute walk away, on the other side of the canal. Daniel knew its concourse, its announcements, the taxi shelter. He circled the station on a printout of the map. Then he added a question mark.

To Shah, he sent a short note. Possible transport announcement. Uncertain destination: Mereford. Noise consistent with a nearby vehicle. Station to check. He made clear that he intended to go there in the morning.

Before closing the computer, he looked at the circle on the map. He had drawn the question mark much smaller. He made it bigger.
