# The Cassette She Kept

Elsie arrived at two o'clock, carrying a pharmacy bag. She kissed Ben. He let her, his arms at his sides. “I should have answered,” she said. “Yes.” She did not add an explanation. Daniel gave them a few minutes by going to fetch a tray.

The bag contained a sealed envelope and a folded sheet of paper. Ruth had written that day's date at the top. She authorised the transfer and examination, as well as the provision of copies to the agreed recipients. A brief exchange with her solicitor had settled the arrangements. Shah knew the cassette was coming. The carrier was to be handed over to her afterwards, with the record of the work performed.

The second half of the page told a different story. “I was afraid of forgetting what I was being asked. I put the recorder on the serving trolley. I didn't say I was recording. It was at my house, after my letter. I kept this cassette with the papers. I took it with me when I left.” Underneath, Ruth had added: “Ben may listen. All of it.”

Daniel photographed the envelope before opening it. Inside, an ordinary sixty-minute cassette lay in a clear case. The label bore the words “home — October”, with no year. He noted the position of the spools and photographed both sides. “Can that give you the date?” Ben asked. “No. The page tells us what Ruth says today. We'll need to compare it.”

Under the light, the shell was not cracked. The visible tape showed no crease or suspicious deposit. Daniel checked the little felt pad that held the tape against the playback head. It was in place. “Are you going to open it?” “Not without a reason.” He took the maintenance log for his deck. That morning's check was already recorded there.

Ben pointed to the small player on a shelf. “Why not that one?” “I like to know what speed it's running at. And to be able to adjust playback.” Daniel connected the deck's outputs to the audio interface. On the computer, he created a folder with a reference number, rather than a suspect's name. Elsie followed his every movement.

“What if it jams?” “I stop.” She nodded. The initial playback check began with a hiss, then a dull thud. A woman said: “In the sitting room, that'll be all right.” Daniel recognised Ruth. A man's voice replied: “Thank you, Mrs Bennett.” “Graham,” Daniel said. “I was with him. I remember this visit.”

The speech remained slightly muffled. Daniel explained what he was going to check before beginning the full transfer. The tape had been recorded on another machine. If the playback head was not correctly aligned, they could lose some of the high frequencies. “The sound comes in crooked?” “The comparison is approximate.” “I hadn't prepared a diagram.” Elsie smiled for the first time.

On that short passage, Daniel checked the azimuth, the angle of the head. He compared the result by listening and using his instruments, then recorded the setting. He would need to return the machine to its reference setting after this cassette. The consonants were a little less dull. The recorded room still sounded distant. “That's better,” Ben said. “A little. I'm not going to fix the recorder's position with a screw.”

Elsie asked whether she could take a photograph to show Ruth. Daniel covered the other clients' files before agreeing. She framed the deck and his hands. “She wants to know whether it'll hold together.” He looked at the cassette. “Tell her I'm watching it.” He did not add a promise.

He let a short passage play to check the level. The peaks remained well below the limit. A voice that was too loud must not overload the digital input. He documented the settings he had chosen, then started again from the beginning. This time, the transfer would preserve everything the machine played.

The first few minutes were full of ordinary things. Graham declined milk. Ruth was looking for the letter she had written. Then Daniel heard his own voice, faster, without the slight effort it took today. “We'll listen together. You'll hear it.” Ben turned his head towards him. Daniel kept his eyes on the counter.

A low hum ran through the recording. It was barely troublesome when Graham spoke near the recorder. But the more distant voices were lost in it. A playback began in the recorded room: thinner speech, coming from another machine. Ruth spoke over it. Daniel made out “I don't”, then nothing certain. His own reply was quiet and fast. He marked the time without stopping playback.

“Can you remove that?” Ben murmured. “Perhaps reduce the hum. Not cleanly separate two people speaking at once.” Daniel picked up his pencil. He wanted to make the software try everything it offered. He only wrote “passage to examine”.

The conversation continued. Ruth asked whether her employer would be told. Graham replied that they would discuss the next steps again. A chair scraped the floor. Ruth said she had to collect Ben. Beside Daniel, the man with that name looked down. After the visitors left, footsteps and the sound of crockery preceded the end of the recording.

Daniel transferred the rest of the side, then the other. It contained an incomplete gardening programme. Elsie left before the end, with a receipt and the promise of a call. Ben stayed. Daniel saved the untreated files and prepared a working copy. He checked that the files opened and that the durations matched his notes. The record kept track of every operation.

Before closing the project, he went back to the first time he spoke. “You'll hear it.” He had asked no question. Ben pulled his sleeve over his watch. “You were sure of yourself.” Daniel stopped playback. “Yes.” That voice belonged to him. He would have preferred to need time to recognise it.
