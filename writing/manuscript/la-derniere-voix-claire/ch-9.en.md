# A Useful Man

Paul Sayer was on the telephone when they arrived. He pointed them towards two armchairs and continued listening. On his desk, a kitchen plan was held flat by a cup. — Then give her the plumber's number, he said. If she cannot call him, you call him. No, we do not leave someone without water until next week. He hung up and pushed aside the plan.

He was about Daniel's age, with grey hair and a shirt with the sleeves rolled up. He stood up to shake Ben's hand. — Benjamin. You look more and more like your mother. — Ben will do. — Of course. Paul turned towards Daniel. — You worked with Graham. — Yes. Paul lowered his voice. — I am sorry.

Daniel thanked him. He already had an answer to that sentence. It came out without his having to prepare it. It was a skill he would have preferred to lose.

The appointment had been arranged the previous evening, after the visit to Ruth's flat. Since then, Ben had received no call from his mother. He had left a second message for Elsie and slept a few hours. He was wearing the same trousers, but a clean jumper. Daniel noticed the jumper and kept that observation to himself.

— I have learned nothing since your call, Paul said. Your mother knew she could come and see me. She still knows. — When did you see her last? Paul looked at his diary. He thought for a moment. — In town, about a fortnight ago. We talked about Graham's book. She did not like the idea of going over everything again.

— Had he sent you pages too? Daniel asked. — No. He had called me. To check dates, company names. — Recently? — Three weeks, perhaps. I had not seen him for months. Paul opened his electronic diary. — I can find the call, if that helps. Daniel said yes.

Ben leaned forward in his armchair. — Did she tell you why she wanted to see Daniel again? — No. She wanted to see him? Daniel explained the missed appointment, without playing the message. Paul listened until the end. — Your mother does not like asking for help. — She did ask for some, Ben said. Paul inclined his head. — You are right.

He offered coffee. Ben declined; Daniel accepted. While the machine heated up, Paul asked what they were looking for in the old papers. Ben looked at Daniel. They had spoken of documents when arranging the appointment. — The circumstances of her testimony, Daniel replied. — She had kept originals? Or copies? — We are establishing that. — Let me know, I may be able to save you looking twice.

The coffee arrived without sugar. Paul asked whether that was all right before sitting down. Daniel appreciated being asked a simple question and having someone wait for the answer. He caught himself relaxing.

— Anna worked for you? — She was self-employed. She checked certain files. Ruth entered the payments, filed the documents. Colin Voss handled the actual accounting. He still has his practice. — And you? — The buildings. The owners. The people with no water. He indicated the telephone with a smile.

Paul moved the cup to show them the kitchen plan. A stain marked its corner. — That was me. I ask for clean documents from everyone, then I do that. He looked for a napkin in a drawer. Ben pushed aside a sheet before the coffee reached it. Paul thanked him.

— The owners paid you for maintenance? Daniel asked. — We managed the money set aside for the work. A roof, communal areas, that sort of thing. Anna matched up what had been ordered, the invoices and the payments. — Three different things, Ben said. — Exactly. Paul looked at Daniel. — You can receive an invoice for something that is not finished. Or pay in instalments. A discrepancy is not always an error.

— And Anna found a lot of them? — She asked a lot of questions. That was her job. He folded up the napkin around the damp patch. — That was also Ruth's job, Ben said. Paul looked up. — Yes. Your mother knew the files.

Ben asked whether the Mill Lane offices still existed. Paul picked up a stiff document wallet from a shelf. He took out of it a plan and a business card. — The upper floor is occupied by Jo Harris. She does picture framing and restoration. I kept the management of the building, not the offices. He called her in front of them. She could see them at half past ten, if the visit did not take too long.

Paul handed them the card. — No need for me to come with you. She knows the building better than I do. If you want plans, I will send you what I have. He closed the wallet and wrote down Daniel's address.

— I imagine you are going to listen to the tape again. — If I can obtain a copy through the proper channels. — An opinion confined to the material supplied. That is more prudent. Daniel put down his cup. — Above all, we will need to know what I am being given. Paul nodded and added a name to the card: Colin Voss.

By the door, he asked Ben whether he still worked for the firm where he had trained. — I am self-employed and have been for six years. — That is good. Your mother must be proud. Ben pulled at his jumper sleeve. — I will ask her.

Outside, Daniel checked the time. They had time to get to Mill Lane on foot. Ben took the card to look at the address. — He gave me a bicycle when I was eight, he said. — That was kind. — Yes. He still asks whether I use it. Daniel looked at his long legs. He had to stop himself smiling.

Paul called them back before they reached the corner. He had forgotten one detail. — About the papers: Anna kept one master set. If you find different versions, check with Colin. He is the one who had the accounting files. He gave them a wave and went back inside. Daniel turned over the card so as not to lose the added name.
