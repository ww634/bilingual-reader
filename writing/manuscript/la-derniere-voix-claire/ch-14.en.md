# No One Has Taken Her

In the car, Ben put his seat belt back on without starting the engine. He was looking at a missed call.

“Elsie. At last,” Ben said.

Daniel placed Judith's folder on his knees. Ben called back. He waited until the call went to voicemail, then tried again. This time, someone answered.

“Where are you?”

Ben listened. His other hand was gripping the steering wheel.

“Since when?”

A van stopped in front of them. The driver asked with gestures whether they were leaving. Daniel shook his head.

“Put her on,” Ben said.

He turned his face towards the window.

“Mum?”

Daniel opened the car door. He waited on the pavement, beside an empty planter. A little soil remained in the corners. He looked at the toes of his shoes while Ben talked. Once, he heard “three nights” through the window.

When Ben got out, he was holding the phone in both hands.

“She's at Elsie's. Since the beginning.”

“Is she all right?” Daniel asked.

“She asked me whether I'd remembered to eat.”

Daniel waited.

“So yes. She's well enough for that.”

For Daniel, relief barely had time to settle before the questions were demanding its seat. Ruth was alive. Ben had just spoken to her. Surely that was where they should start.

“At least…”

Ben looked up.

“Don't finish that.”

Daniel closed his mouth.

Elsie had received Ben's messages. She had also found the ones from the police when she got home. Ruth had asked her for a day without questions. Then another. The previous evening, Elsie had refused to go on. She had called the police back and insisted that Ruth speak to her son.

“She said she wanted to see a solicitor first,” Ben said.

“Has she seen one?”

“This morning. And now she'd like me to be calm.”

Ben rubbed his eyes with one palm.

“I was looking in ditches when I drove.”

Daniel looked at the road. He had no better ending to offer for his sentence.

They went back to the workshop. Ben did not want tea. He sent the time of the call and Elsie's number to the officer handling the missing-person report. Shortly before noon, the officer called him back. Colleagues had seen Ruth and spoken to her alone. She was staying there of her own accord. She had given permission for Ben to be reassured. He thanked the officer, without asking another question.

Daniel took out three plates by mistake. He put one back. He had only bread, cheese and an overripe tomato. He left the tomato in the kitchen.

“I can drive you to Mereford,” Daniel said.

“She wants to see me there tomorrow morning. Alone.”

“All right.”

“She doesn't want to see you.”

Daniel cut an overthick slice of bread.

“I'd gathered that.”

He had imagined Ruth sitting opposite him, glad that he had found the phone box. He would have opened his notebook. They would have gone through the facts. In that scene, he had not allowed for her refusing the chair.

Ben took the piece of bread and broke it in half.

“She got on a coach,” he said. “To Mereford.”

Daniel pictured again the three bays, the loudspeaker against the office.

“Did she give you a time?” Daniel asked.

“I didn't ask for the service number.”

Daniel put down his slice of bread.

“Sorry.”

Ben shook his head.

“No. I know why you're asking. But I'm not there yet.”

Ruth had explained to him that she really had intended to see Daniel. After leaving the message, she had changed her mind. She knew Ben was supposed to meet her. She had left anyway.

“She says she was going to call back.”

“What stopped her?” Daniel asked.

“She did.”

The bread cracked between Ben's fingers.

“That's what she said. Not a fault. Not someone outside the door.”

Daniel gathered a few crumbs with the side of his hand. He had devoted hours to the call. He had searched for what was happening around Ruth, behind her, further along the street. The recording had told him nothing about what she had decided afterwards. He wished Ben's fear had a stop button. He had all this equipment, and nothing for that. Ben brushed the remaining crumbs onto his plate.

“She told me she was ashamed. I asked her of what,” Ben said.

“And?”

“She wants to tell me in person.”

At twenty past twelve, Elsie called Ben a second time. She asked to speak to Daniel.

“I have a cassette,” Elsie said. “Ruth brought it with her.”

Daniel put down the bread knife.

“The one from the empty sleeve?” Daniel asked.

“She says so. She's willing to let you listen to it. Not to have you call her.”

They discussed the case and the label, then the handover. Daniel asked for written permission and the contact details for Ruth's solicitor. They needed to agree what he would do and who would receive copies.

“She's written something,” Elsie said. “I'll bring it.”

Her voice changed when she asked to speak to Ben again. Daniel handed back the phone and moved away.

Later, Ben ate a slice of cheese on its own.

“I didn't say I was glad to hear her.”

Daniel waited.

“I was.”

“You can write to her,” Daniel replied.

“That'll make her think everything's all right.”

Daniel pushed the plate towards him.

“Then write both.”

Ben picked up his phone. He typed for a long time, deleted some of it, then sent his message. He put the phone back on the workbench, with the screen facing up. For the first time since they had met, he did not keep his hand on it.
