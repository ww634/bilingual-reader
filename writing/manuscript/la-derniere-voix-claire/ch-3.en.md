# A Fall on the Stairs

The police called back at ten to five. The woman on the phone introduced herself: Leila Shah, detective inspector. She wanted to come and see him. Daniel gave her the address, then specified which door to use. He was explaining where to park when he realised that he was delaying the end of the call. Once alone, he would have to wait again.

He washed both cups, including the one that had not been used. He put away the cheese. On the table, the bag of screws was still there. He put it in a drawer, then took it out again. Otherwise, he would never find it again.

Shah arrived with a colleague a little after five. She was wearing a blue coat, one sleeve of which was wet. She checked his identity and asked him whether he would prefer to sit in the house. He chose the workshop. In the kitchen, he would have had to move Marianne's things on the third chair. It was only a shopping bag. It had been there for four years.

The colleague took the stool. Daniel brought a chair from the kitchen for Shah, the one with nothing on it. She thanked him without commenting on this small rearrangement.

— Judith told me there were police officers at her house. — Yes. — She did not tell me what had happened. Shah waited until he looked at her. — Mr Ellis was found at the foot of the cellar stairs. For now, we cannot treat his death as an accident.

Daniel knew those stairs. Graham kept wine down there, which he bought by the case to save money. Then he would explain to his guests that they had to drink it to make room. Daniel looked at Shah's hands. She had put down her pen.

— Do you think that someone… — We are trying to understand what happened. There are aspects that concern us. I cannot tell you more today.

He would have liked a sentence that was more precise. He knew why she was not giving him one. For years, he had appreciated that caution in other people.

She asked him to go through his day from the beginning. He spoke about Mrs Holt, the hardware shop, the message. He showed the call times on his mobile. Shah noted down twelve seventeen. — And your last conversation with him, before today? He had to look. They did not call each other every day. He had never wondered whether that was a failing.

Shah asked him whether Graham had recently changed his habits. Daniel thought of saying no. Then he thought about the lunches he himself had cancelled. He knew the version of Graham that reached the workshop. That was not his whole life. — We saw each other less often, he said. — Since when? — Since my wife died. Shah's colleague looked up. Daniel gave the year to avoid being offered condolences for the second time in the same conversation.

Graham sometimes came round without warning. He brought something that needed repairing. A speaker, a radio, once a desk lamp. Daniel had explained to him that a lamp was not an audio device. Graham had clicked the switch beside his ear. He had found his demonstration sufficient.

— He came for the repairs? The question was gentle, almost practical. Daniel looked at the lamp, at the end of the workbench. — I told him I would bring it back to him. Shah did not repeat the question. The colleague turned the page in his notebook. Daniel waited for them to ask him something else.

The last call was from the previous Sunday. Graham wanted to know whether he had a stepladder. Daniel had advised him to pay someone. Graham had replied that he was looking for a stepladder, not a financial adviser. Shah gave Daniel time to finish.

Then they listened to Ruth. Daniel pressed the button and took away his hand. Nobody spoke until the time announcement. On the second listen, Shah asked her colleague to note down the final words exactly.

— She asked you to listen to her before speaking to Graham. — Yes. — You called him straight away. — I thought he would know why she was coming back. She wrote his answer. That was more unpleasant than if she had challenged it.

He told her about the book. He found the September email and forwarded it to the address she gave him. — He could be tactless, he said. But he did not want to hurt people. — Who was supposed to read those pages? — I do not know. The answer struck him as disloyal. It was accurate, though.

Shah asked him whether he knew Graham's regular visitors. They were checking who had come by, the calls, what the neighbours had noticed. — Did he receive deliveries in the evening? — Wine, sometimes. Why? — A delivery vehicle was reported in the street. We are trying to find the driver. He thought of the cases in the cellar and said nothing more.

As for Ruth, he had neither an address nor a number to give them. He described his unsuccessful searches. Shah asked whether he knew her family. — She had a little boy. I did not meet him. As he said it, he realised that this boy was now over thirty.

They would try to contact Ruth, Shah said. She also wanted to have the message preserved and check where the call came from. Daniel showed her the machine. No file to send from a menu. No number on the display. — That does not mean it does not exist elsewhere, she pointed out. — I know. — Then we agree.

A colleague would come to collect the machine that evening. Until then, she asked him not to delete anything and not to change the settings. Daniel offered a recording of the playback, in the meantime. Shah agreed, on condition that he explain how he had made it. That copy would not replace the machine.

Before leaving, she returned to the Vale case. — Did you meet Mrs Bennett at the time? — Once. At her home, with Graham. I played her passages that I had processed. — Why? — To explain what could be heard. He had already given himself that answer. It sounded less convincing out loud.

— Her account changed after that playback? — It became clearer. Shah did not look down at her notes. Daniel heard his own answer, with a slight delay. He had spoken as though she had accused him of something.
