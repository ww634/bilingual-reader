# Someone Else Looking

The young man turned around. He had red eyes and a paint mark on his knee. His phone dropped against his thigh.

“Are you with the police?” the young man asked.

“No,” Daniel replied.

“Then why are you stopping when I say her name?”

Daniel moved forward to let through a customer behind him. She wanted milk. She looked as though she needed it more than he needed a dramatic entrance.

“She called me yesterday,” Daniel said.

“When?”

“At eleven fifty-two.”

The young man raised his head.

“Did you see her?”

“No. She left a message.”

The answer changed his face more than Daniel would have wished. He must have believed, for a second, that someone was bringing him his mother.

“Ben Bennett,” the young man said.

“Daniel.”

Ben waited for more. Daniel pointed towards the phone box.

“I think she may have called from there.”

“You think, or you know?”

“I think.”

“All right. Keep that word.”

The shopkeeper took payment for the milk. His name was Owen; his name appeared on a small badge pinned to his cardigan.

“The telephone works,” Owen said. “Someone asked me again for change on Monday.”

“And yesterday?” Daniel asked.

“Yesterday, I worked eleven hours,” Owen replied.

There was no hostility in his answer. It was a unit of measurement.

Ben put away his phone.

“Mr Owen, yesterday, you told me that you had a delivery around midday. Were you in the stockroom the whole time?” Ben asked.

“No, actually. One crate was missing. I was waiting for him to find it,” Owen said.

He looked towards the door, as though the delivery driver was still there.

“A woman was in the phone box. A light-coloured coat. She had a bag,” Owen said.

“Do you remember anything else?” Ben asked.

“I mostly thought that she was going to miss her bus. She was looking outside. But I do not know which one she wanted to catch,” Owen replied.

Daniel wanted to ask whether she seemed afraid. He looked at the distance between the till and the phone box. From there, he could make out the receiver, not the buttons.

“Did you see her face?” Daniel asked.

“Not clearly,” Owen replied.

Mr Owen looked at Ben.

“I would like to tell you yes.”

“I know,” Ben replied.

Ben asked whether he still had the delivery note. Perhaps the time would make it possible to narrow down his recollection. Mr Owen took out a folder from beneath the counter. The receipt bore a signature, no time. Ben did not ask him to invent one. He noted down the supplier's name to pass it on to the police.

Mr Owen closed the folder, then opened it again.

“I do not want anyone writing that I recognised her,” Owen said.

Ben showed him his screen. He had typed: “A woman, identity unconfirmed.”

“Is that all right?”

The shopkeeper nodded. Daniel looked at the young man a little differently. He knew what he wanted and managed not to ask for it.

“Which way did she go?” Daniel asked.

“I have no idea. The driver had found the crate. I had to sign,” Owen replied.

The end of the recollection was there, in a task more urgent than a stranger on the telephone. Ben thanked the shopkeeper. He did not ask him to try again.

On the way out, he bought a bottle of water.

“The timer is still holding up?” Ben asked.

“Yes. It is the rest that is wearing out,” Owen replied.

Ben smiled without pressing. Daniel followed him under the awning.

Outside, Ben reopened Ruth's photograph. Daniel recognised the line of her mouth. The rest had changed, as his own face must have changed. He remembered dark hair. The hair in the photograph was grey, cut short.

“Is it recent?” Daniel asked.

“June. My birthday,” Ben replied.

On the cake, the candles formed the number thirteen. Daniel looked at them.

“Thirty-one,” Ben said. “She had reversed the numbers.”

“A mistake?” Daniel asked.

“With her, you should never jump to conclusions,” Ben replied.

He moved on to the next photograph. In it, Ruth was holding a cake knife and pointing at the numbers with exaggerated seriousness. Ben closed the photographs. Daniel had never seen that expression on the face of the woman sitting in front of his player.

“Do you know him?” Daniel asked.

“I redid some of the lighting last winter. I am an electrician.”

He unscrewed the bottle cap and drank half.

“You, what do you do?”

“I work on recordings.”

“All right. And that brings you here?”

Daniel explained the announcement, the noise, the station where he had gone wrong. He showed the photograph he had just taken of the phone box number. Ben took out his phone. He had the same photograph, taken the day before.

“We could put on an exhibition,” Ben said.

“Two photographs is not many.”

“It depends on the price of admission.”

Daniel put away his phone.

“Were you waiting for her here?”

Ben pointed at the shelter on the other side of the bays.

“Over there. Yesterday, at half past twelve. She had called me that morning. She wanted to give me something. I was on a job. I arrived ten minutes late.”

He tightened the cap until the plastic creaked.

“Ten minutes,” Daniel repeated.

“I know. You are going to tell me that it is not my fault.”

“I was going to ask you whether she had specified what she wanted to give you.”

Ben loosened his grip.

“Some papers. I said I could come round to her place. She preferred here,” Ben said.

He had waited, called, then gone to her home. That evening, he had reported her disappearance. He had given the meeting place and the phone box number. Daniel told him about Shah, then about Graham.

“Graham Ellis is dead. The police are trying to understand what happened,” Daniel said.

Ben stopped watching the buses.

“The one writing the book?”

“Yes.”

“My mother knows?”

“I do not know.”

Ben looked at the phone box, then Shah's number on the card that Daniel was holding out to him. He took a breath before dialling. They called the number she had left and passed on both reference numbers. Ben described what Mr Owen had just said. Daniel made clear that the shopkeeper had already seen Ruth's photograph. The photograph had preceded the question; that needed to be in the account.

After the call, Ben looked at the buses.

“I called her friends, her neighbour, my mother's cousin, Elsie. I left messages everywhere. She could have left me one.”

A bus left bay three. Ben watched it go. He had probably done that all the previous day.

“Do you have a card?” Ben asked.

Daniel found one in his wallet. Ben read the name, then the line beneath it. Audio restoration. He did not put away the card.

“Mercer,” Ben said.

Daniel waited.

“It was you, on the recording. The Vale case.”

“I worked on it, yes.”

Ben turned over the card, as though the back might contain another explanation.

“My mother called you,” Ben said.

This time, it was not a question.
