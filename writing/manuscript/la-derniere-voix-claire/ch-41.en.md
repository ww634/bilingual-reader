# Epilogue — The Words That Are There

Eighteen months later, Mrs Holt came back with a cassette labelled “Kitchen 96”. Her father was trying to explain a recipe on it. A child interrupted to clarify that he had never made that cake on his own. He protested, without asking for a stop to the recording.

“He wanted to show my sister how it was done,” Mrs Holt said. “She lived a long way away. We used to send her cassettes.”

The transfer was complete. Daniel had kept both channels in the preservation file. On the right-hand one, a clatter of crockery masked a quantity. On the left, the voice was quieter, but so was the clatter. He played the channels separately, at comparable levels.

“There,” Mrs Holt said. “That's almost it.”

Daniel replayed the whole sentence. He could distinguish the beginning of a number, then the scrape of a container across the table. He turned down the volume. His ears were beginning to prefer one answer simply because they expected it.

“I can choose this channel for the listening copy. The complete file will remain intact. As for the number, I'm going to leave an uncertainty marker in the text.”

Mrs Holt looked at the transcript.

“That makes an odd recipe.”

“There's also no oven temperature.”

She laughed. Daniel let the playback continue. Mrs Holt's father was asking where his wife had put the tin. The child told him it was in front of him. For a few seconds, the client stopped looking at the screen.

After she left, Daniel noted the channel choice and closed the working copy. The inner door opened without his lifting it. Ben had finally adjusted it, with his permission. Daniel still sometimes lifted the handle before pushing. His hand still raised objections the wood had abandoned.

The pane in the courtyard door had been replaced once the workshop was handed back to Daniel. At first, Daniel had heard every movement in the passage. He still looked up more often than he used to. He had resumed appointments, at first in the mornings, then at times that suited other people.

He had also returned to his old cases. The documents arrived through the agreed channels, with gaps that needed identifying. He had learned to write “not available” without turning it into “unimportant”. Each answer bore a date; some questions had several.

Paul's trial had taken place before that winter. Daniel had given evidence about his old work, about his review and about the workshop. He had had to answer the question he had long reduced to a matter of settings. Why had he allowed people to believe that Ruth had recognised the first name without his help? He had answered without asking for help finishing.

A jury had found Paul guilty of all three murders. The proceedings had placed Paul's visit to Mill Lane in the context of Anna's work on the payments. Anna had been strangled. Graham's fall had concealed an assault. Ruth had been killed after choosing to give her account. The financial documents, the movements, the findings and the witness accounts had been examined, challenged, compared. The first name on A remained uncertain. No new word had needed to appear on the tape.

Michael's case had followed a separate route. After the referral by the CCRC, the Court of Appeal had quashed his conviction without ordering a retrial. Helen had called Daniel after the hearing. Michael had been released. Daniel's first question had been to ask whether he had somewhere to go. Helen had told him that it had been arranged. He had not offered to come and collect him.

Michael had received the photograph of Anna. He had sent his thanks to Claire. Daniel knew that, and that his old report remained in the papers he would need to explain. His correction had been added, not substituted.

Ben had attended several days of the trial. On other days, he had gone to work. Sometimes he had let Daniel know he would not come on Thursday. Daniel had learned not to ask for a justification for every absence. Ben also had friends, clients, a flat where the charger stayed plugged in.

During the first months, he had sometimes asked Daniel to go with him to Ruth's building. Daniel waited where Ben asked him to. Once, they had stayed for ten minutes in the car, then left. Another time, Ben had brought a box from the flat. Daniel did not know what was in the boxes that remained at Elsie's. He knew that Ben had opened some of them, and changed his mind about others.

That Thursday, Ben arrived with bread and a jumper that smelled of paint. He had had to move a shelf to reach a box. The client had omitted to mention that the shelf was freshly painted.

“He told me to be careful,” Ben said. “Afterwards.”

Daniel looked at the sleeve.

“He chose a colour that suits you.”

“It was the good news I'd been waiting for.”

Ben put down the bread and looked at the clock.

“Have you eaten?”

“I was waiting for you.”

They took out the plates. Ben knew where they were; he still asked before moving the equipment. The second chair was used often enough that they had had to replace the cushion. Daniel had chosen a fabric on which tea would show less. Ben had called that prediction offensive, then reasonable.

During dinner, he spoke about his mother while describing a journey they used to make when he was little. She got off one stop before the right one, to go past a shop window. He had never known what she was looking at. The shop had closed before he asked the question. Daniel did not choose an answer for him. He asked him which street it was in.

Ben helped himself to more potatoes without breaking off his answer. Daniel hoped there would be some potatoes left for him.

The following morning, Daniel went to the appointment arranged a week earlier. An investigator was waiting for him with Nisha. He was being asked to contribute as a witness to a check on the origin of the instructions sent to Paul. He had already supplied his initial account and the references of the documents he had in mind.

The voice message came from Paul's telephone, seized when he was arrested. It had been examined during the investigation. This session concerned its connection with the old correspondence and the requests made to Daniel. Nisha had examined the audio copy under the terms of an authorised assignment. The device would not be given to Daniel.

The checks placed receipt of the message before the call at four thirty-two, on the day Ruth had died. The sender's identity had not been established.

Nisha started the playback. The words were intelligible without cleaning. The voice spoke without hurrying.

“Stop justifying yourself to Mercer. The wording is ‘an opinion limited to the material submitted’. He'll look for a limitation he can defend. Get him to write it down. No context in the statement. We can still protect you. But we need his signature.”

Daniel waited for the file to finish. He did not recognise the voice. He recognised the work they wanted him to do.

He indicated the letter of 19 October 2001, then his note about Paul's call. The wording was already there before his report. The question about the generation of a copy had also been forwarded to Paul; he had read it without being able to explain it.

“Do you think this person was already involved in 2001?” the investigator asked.

“I don't know. The wording could have circulated. We need to trace who supplied it, and to whom.”

He asked that his supplementary account distinguish what he had heard that morning from what he knew before entering. The word “we” was in the message. It said nothing about the person using it, or the means at their disposal.

The investigator specified what he could tell Ben: they were checking the source of advice received by Paul, relating to the request for a signature. The file remained within the investigation.

That evening, Daniel gave him that summary. Ben asked whether he could help.

“To go over the references, yes. As for the documents, I'm going to ask whom to approach.”

Ben pulled up the second chair. Daniel opened his notebook at a fresh page. He wrote the first question he would send.

“Who supplied the proposed transcript with the request of 19 October?”
