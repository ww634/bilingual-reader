# The Terms

Ben chose the café across the street. He did not ask Daniel whether he had time. Daniel followed him. He had a message from a client on his mobile, two invoices to send, and no honest way to prefer the invoices.

They sat down beside the window. Ben put Daniel's card between the sugar and the menu. — Tell me what you did. — On the Vale case? — Yes. Not just your title on the card.

The waitress came to take their order. Daniel asked for tea. Ben asked for a coffee, then a sandwich. He looked at Daniel. — Do you want one? — No, thank you. — As you like. He did not insist. Daniel was grateful to him for that.

— At the time, I worked as a civilian audio specialist for the police. I was given a recording made in Anna Vale's office. You could hear an argument. Some of the words were difficult to understand. — And you made them clear. — I tried to make them more intelligible. I also wrote a transcript. There was disagreement about a name.

— Michael. Daniel nodded. — The husband. — They were separated. — Does that change what you heard? — No. He had begun with a detail that did not answer anything. Ben did not need someone to correct his family vocabulary.

Daniel began again. He had proposed a reading of the passage. The defence had challenged it. The jury had also heard the testimony about the couple, about Michael's visits, and Ruth's account. The recording had not constituted the whole case.

— You are telling me that it was not just you. Daniel moved the spoon on his saucer. The waitress had just put down the drinks. — I am telling you what was presented. But yes. That is also what I am trying to tell you. Ben looked at him a little longer. Then he pulled his plate towards him.

— Did you know my mother? — I met her at her home, with Graham Ellis. We played her passages. — Had she asked you to come? Daniel searched his memory. He could see the tablecloth and the player, the extension lead he had needed to use. He could not see the conversation that had arranged the visit. — I no longer remember. Graham had arranged it.

Ben cut his sandwich in half. — His name was in the pages she received. Yours too. — Graham's memoir? — A few sheets, in an envelope. She showed them to me last week. She said: “He tells it as though I knew everything.”

The tea was still too hot. Daniel put down the cup without drinking. — She was upset by the way he portrayed her. — Maybe. That is not what she said. Ben had not raised his voice. Daniel felt that he needed to stop before explaining Ben's own mother to him.

— What was worrying you, these last few days? Ben looked outside. — She was not calling back straight away. Well, that, she is entitled to do. But when she did call back, she began by asking who had spoken to me. She cancelled a lunch on Sunday. She had made the whole meal, then she said she was tired. — Did she tell you about a threat? — No. I have already told the police.

His phone vibrated on the table. He read the message, then typed a reply. — A client. I found someone to finish what I left yesterday. He is very pleased to pay another electrician to understand what I did. — Do you have notes? — Yes, Daniel. I have notes. He pressed send. Daniel drank his tea. This time, he could do so.

— And her mobile? — At her place. On the charger. I heard it ring when I arrived. I have a key. I thought she was in the bathroom. He looked at the coffee he had barely touched. — Perhaps she forgot it, Daniel said. — She left a note beside it.

Ben found a photograph on his phone. Two lines, on the back of an envelope. “I am leaving my mobile here. I need to think.” There was neither a name nor a time. — The police have seen it? — Yes. They came last night. They also saw the charger. I have not kept the important things for you.

Daniel handed him back the phone. The sentence made the departure more deliberate. It said nothing about what had happened afterwards. — She could have let you know from the phone box. — Yes, Ben said. He pushed his plate a few centimetres away. Half the sandwich remained.

Shah called back shortly afterwards. Daniel asked whether he could put her on speaker, with Ben. They went outside so as not to impose the conversation on the neighbouring tables. She had both their reports. The phone box number would be checked against the call data. She could not yet confirm that Ruth had used it. Someone would speak to the shopkeeper.

Ben asked her whether Graham's death was connected with his mother. He had learned the news from Daniel outside the shop. Shah did not turn their concern into a conclusion. She wanted any new information, even if it seemed ordinary. If Ruth contacted them, they were to let her know.

Daniel mentioned Graham's pages. Ben explained that his mother had asked him the previous week also to read a blue folder containing her old letters. He had not made time. She had told him he could take it if he preferred to read it at home. The folder was on the sitting-room table the previous evening.

Shah asked them not to move anything for the moment. She would arrange a check of what had already been recorded in the flat. They would then agree on limited access to those papers, without touching anything else. Ben's key did not give them the right to search through Ruth's whole life. Ben said he understood.

When they went back inside, the waitress had stacked their cups. The half sandwich was waiting in a paper bag. Ben thanked her and took out his bank card. Daniel paid for his own tea. Neither of them offered to pay for the other.

— I want to hear her message, Ben said. — I can play it for you. — All of it. Daniel looked at the card that Ben was still holding. — All of it.

They found a bench away from the bus exit. Daniel had the unprocessed copy on his phone. He explained how he had made it and where the answering machine was now. Then he started playback from the beginning. Ben held the phone close to his ear, without touching the screen.

Daniel heard his first name, then Ruth's. The Anna Vale case. The appointment. “Before speaking to Graham.” Ben did not look up. He waited until the end, the sentence about what she had not been so sure of, then the time. He handed back the phone to Daniel.

— She had said two o'clock for you. — Yes. — For me, half past twelve. He looked at the bag in which his sandwich was cooling. — She had planned to see both of us. Daniel let him finish his thought. — Then something changed. He did not say “someone stopped her”. Daniel did not know whether he had the strength or whether he simply had a need to believe it.

On the pavement, Ben finally put away the card. — I am not promising you that I will trust you. — I did not ask you to. — And you tell me when you do not know. Daniel thought of the station, the circle, the little question mark. — Agreed.

— Right, Mr Mercer… — Daniel. — Are you sure? — On that point, yes. Ben almost smiled. His phone vibrated. A message from Shah's team gave them an appointment at the flat at one o'clock, about the papers. Ben looked at the time. — I should have read them when she asked me to. Daniel had several reassuring answers. He kept them to himself.
