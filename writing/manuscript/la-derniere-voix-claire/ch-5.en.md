# The Wrong Station

The next morning, Daniel picked up the wrong jacket. He realised it on the canal bridge, when he looked for his gloves. The right one contained them, along with the printed map. He kept walking. He knew the way to the station. It was precisely what worried him a little.

At nine, the concourse smelled of hot coffee and damp coats. A man was pulling a suitcase, one wheel of which struck every join in the tiles. Daniel stepped aside out of the way and waited for an announcement. He had left his headphones at home. This time, the task was to listen to the place.

The voice came from above him, preceded by three notes. A train was announced for Mereford. For a moment, Daniel felt the simple satisfaction of being in the right place. Then the announcement ended. Outside, a coach pulled away. Daniel saw its exhaust before hearing its engine.

The glass doors were closed. The noise remained far beyond them. This was not proof. Ruth could have stood somewhere else, near an open door. But the picture he had formed of her in this concourse was beginning to require a lot of exceptions.

He went out onto the forecourt. There, he could hear the engines, but almost none of the announcements. He followed the front of the building as far as the taxi shelter. No public telephone. He had no reason to suppose that she had used one, anyway. She could have called from a mobile. On his sheet of paper, he had already built a station. He needed to avoid adding a phone box to it just to fill the space.

Near the ticket barriers, a member of staff approached him.

“Are you waiting for someone?” the member of staff asked.

Daniel almost replied yes. Since the day before, he had done nothing else.

“I am looking for a woman who may have called from here yesterday. The police know about it,” Daniel said.

The member of staff asked him whether he had a photograph. He did not. He could describe a woman twenty-five years younger, sitting in a kitchen. He could also recite her message. Neither of those skills helped the man in front of him.

“For the camera footage, you need to go through our manager,” the member of staff said.

He gave him a contact to pass on to the investigators. Daniel noted it down without asking for an exception. In the past, Graham's name would have shortened the conversation. He was not going to use it today.

On the forecourt, he found a bench and went back through his notes. Three columns, this time: heard, assumed, to check. The word “station” did not belong in the first. He moved it. On paper, the word was easy to move. In his head, it had already laid foundations.

At the information desk, an employee was helping a woman find a ticket on her phone. Daniel waited his turn. When he explained that he was looking for a place from which an announcement could be heard, the employee pointed at the ceiling.

“Here, it works quite well,” the woman at the information desk said.

“An announcement for Mereford, with traffic right beside it,” Daniel replied.

“Are you looking for a train or a bus?”

“That is a good question.”

She looked at him with a patience he had earned.

The coaches that stopped outside the station left without an outdoor announcement, she explained. At the old bus station, however, the small office still used a loudspeaker for changes of bay. The Mereford service stopped there. There were roadworks this week. Departures often changed sides.

“The old bus station is still open?” Daniel asked.

“You know, ‘old’ is mostly just a name.”

She turned over a map and showed him the route. Twelve minutes walking down towards the canal, then right, before the bridge. Daniel thanked her. He took the map. This time, nobody had circled the answer in advance.

Outside, he sent a correction to Shah. The concourse did not fit well. He was going to check the bus station. He hesitated before sending it, then grew annoyed at that hesitation. The day before, he had promised a hypothesis. He could hardly blame the world for being uncooperative.

As he walked downhill, the shop windows became smaller. A hairdresser's, a phone repair shop, an empty shop still promising to open soon. The word “soon” had yellowed in the sun. He knew this part of Calderwick through his car window. On foot, he discovered the slope and the smell of bread behind a service door.

The bus station occupied a triangle of tarmac between the canal and a row of shops. Three bays, a low shelter, a glazed office. Two orange barriers blocked access to the first bay. On the office roof, a loudspeaker pointed towards the passengers.

Daniel heard it before reaching the shelter. The next departure for Mereford would leave from bay three. A woman picked up her bags with a sigh. A bus was passing between Daniel and the office. The end of the announcement disappeared behind the engine.

He stopped. It was not the same sound. He was listening with his own ears, without a telephone or answering machine between him and the street. But the two events could happen together, here, without a complicated explanation. An announcement. A nearby vehicle. He had a place to examine, not a proven location.

The phone box stood at the end of the row of shops. It was not red. Glass, a metal frame, a replacement pane clearer than the others. Daniel first thought that it served as an advertising board. Then he saw the receiver behind the glass.

He did not open the door. If Ruth had called from here, it was better to let the police decide what to examine. He photographed the location and the displayed number without touching anything. From there, one could see the loudspeaker above the office. The bus exit lane ran a few metres away.

He was about to go into the small shop next door to ask whether the telephone worked. A voice stopped him.

“I already showed you the photograph yesterday,” the young man said.

Through the open door, he saw a young man in work trousers, holding out his phone. On the screen, a woman was laughing in front of a cake. Daniel did not recognise her at once.

“Her name is Ruth Bennett,” the young man said.

The shopkeeper looked over the young man's shoulder. Daniel had remained on the threshold.
