# An Earlier Visit

Daniel arrived at the workshop with five minutes to spare. Ben had gone to collect some equipment from a supplier. He had asked whether he could come back afterwards. Daniel had said yes without checking his appointment book. He checked it as he put down his keys. No client was coming. He no longer had a practical excuse. The others would have required a little honesty.

Helen Ward called at the agreed time. Her first question concerned Daniel's work in 2001. He specified what he had examined, the report he had written and the session at Ruth's home. She did not interrupt him. When he finished, she asked what had changed.

“A former witness wanted me to listen to something. I have not received that recording. I only have her message and an old letter expressing reservations,” Daniel said.

“Are you questioning your conclusion?” Helen asked.

He looked at the drawer where he kept his old notebooks.

“I want to review what it was based on.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

She explained to him that she had not defended Michael at trial. She had taken over his case later, after an unsuccessful appeal. Michael continued to dispute his conviction. That work did not consist of sending the same letter every year with the date changed. They needed something new that genuinely mattered.

“The CCRC can examine a disputed conviction,” Helen said. “But an application is not an acquittal. And the concern of a former expert is not yet new evidence.”

Daniel wrote the initials on his sheet, then “independent review”. Helen Ward continued before he asked the question.

“You can explain your work. You cannot be alone in deciding whether it was right,” Helen said.

He told her about Ruth's letter, separating its exact terms from his own comments. The solicitor wanted to verify its provenance and compare it with the material from the time. They agreed to arrange that transfer with the people concerned. Then she asked what he remembered of the rest of the case.

Daniel mentioned the separation, the threats, the lie about a visit. The headlines came back more readily than the dates. They had larger type. Helen Ward brought him back to what Michael had admitted. He had gone to see Anna earlier on the day of her death. He had asked her for money to settle a debt. She had refused. He had lost his temper.

“He had said he had not been there,” Daniel said.

“At first, yes,” Helen replied.

“That did not help,” Daniel replied.

The silence changed on the telephone. He heard the rustle of a sheet.

“I have a statement he authorised me to share for requests to review his case. I am going to read you a passage,” Helen said.

“I lied about the visit. I was ashamed of having asked her for money after what I had said to her. I hit her door with my hand. She asked me to leave. I left. You can write that I frightened her. Do not write that I killed her because it makes a neater ending to the story.”

Daniel said nothing. This was not Michael's voice. It was a solicitor reading words chosen to be read. That did not make the sentence about the door any easier to hear. He could see Anna behind that door, then the man who had left her. He could not turn them into a simple couple to make his work easier.

The solicitor resumed the points in the case. The visit explained some traces of his presence. It did not give a time to all those traces. The prosecution had linked the threats, the lie, Ruth's account and the supposed words on the tape. The defence had challenged that reading. The jury had convicted Michael.

“He still says that he did not return that evening?” Daniel asked.

“Yes. And we have to test his account, not simply prefer it,” Helen replied.

Daniel thought of the rectangular folder in Ruth's letter.

“Did he have anything with him on the first visit?” Daniel asked.

She consulted another document.

“His work bag, according to his account. A soft bag with a strap. That is not an inventory of everything he might have carried that day,” Helen said.

Daniel wrote down the answer. It was not enough to replace one man with a bag to solve the case. But he had two descriptions to check, at two different times.

They returned to the recording. Helen Ward made clear that the defence had had access to a complete copy at the time. The selected passage was not everything that existed.

“Do you want my old extract?” Daniel asked.

“I want to know how you arrived at that extract. And obtain an appropriate copy of the source for a fresh examination,” Helen replied.

“Your first transcript,” Helen Ward asked, “have you still got it?”

“I have my notes. I need to check which versions I kept,” Daniel replied.

“Check. Also keep anything that does not match your recollection,” Helen replied.

Daniel put his hand on the drawer. He could already imagine the neatly arranged file, the dead ends erased, every page politely pointing to the next.

He remembered changing the punctuation between two playbacks. A full stop becoming a question mark. On paper, that changed the person to whom the sentence seemed addressed. He no longer remembered which version had come first. He said so to Helen Ward. She asked him to note it down as a recollection, until he found the sheets.

“When you have the copy, will you be able to tell me what he actually said?” Helen asked.

Daniel almost began with the name of a piece of software. He looked at the apple on the sheet.

“I will be able to examine the sound. I cannot promise you a sentence,” Daniel said.

“That is the answer I will pass on.”

She sounded neither pleased nor disappointed. He was not there to reassure her.

She would discuss with Michael the role that Daniel could take in that process. She would arrange the requests for material and the conditions of its examination. Nothing would be available that afternoon. As for a visit, she would check what was possible and what her client wanted. Daniel said he understood. He had almost said that he was in no hurry.

“There is another sentence in his statement,” Helen Ward said. “It is addressed to the people who come back to reconsider their work.”

Daniel stopped writing.

“‘I do not need to be told that someone is sorry. What are you prepared to put in writing now?’” Helen read.

Daniel's pen remained above the sheet. He did not ask the solicitor to repeat it.
