# Graham's Version

Judith opened before the second ring. She was wearing one of Graham's shirts, with the sleeves rolled up.

“Put it here,” Judith said, pointing to a small table.

Daniel put the lamp down. The base left a circle in the dust.

“It works,” Daniel said.

“I'd gathered that. Thank you.”

Ben was waiting behind him, with his coat still buttoned. Judith asked him his name, then showed them into the sitting room. On the sofa, three shopping bags held envelopes containing sympathy cards. A cold cup of tea stood on Graham's armchair. Daniel almost moved it. He kept his hands in his pockets.

“The copies are there. Not the rest,” Judith said.

Judith pointed to a folder on the table.

“The inspector already has all this. I made a list of what I'm giving you.”

“You didn't have to…”

“I know.”

She sat down without offering them tea.

Graham printed his drafts on paper that had already been used. Daniel recognised, on the back of one page, the programme for a dinner they had attended together. He remembered the overcooked fish and Graham asking for bread. He could no longer remember what they had been celebrating. The fish had lasted better than the occasion.

The first version presented the Vale case as a difficult investigation conducted patiently. In it Graham described a team that had persevered. In the margin, more recent handwriting asked: “Persevered against what?” Daniel brought the page closer to the window.

“He'd begun to have doubts,” Daniel said.

“He'd begun to rewrite,” Judith said. “That's what I know.”

Daniel looked at her.

“Do you think he was trying to protect himself?”

“I think you'd like a simple answer before you've even read it.”

Ben had picked up another page.

“Here, he writes that my mother was categorical.”

Beneath the sentence, Graham had added: “At what point? Go back to the earliest records.” Daniel asked whether there was a date for that correction. Judith shook her head.

“He moved between versions. And he lost his glasses. I can give you a date for that: every day.”

The folder also contained copies of old letters. A commissioning letter bore the department's reference and Daniel's name. Subject, enclosures, requested deadline. Then, on a separate line: “An opinion limited to the material submitted.” He had heard the same wording at Paul's office. He checked the list of enclosures: a recording, a proposed transcript. The transcript was not attached to this copy. He made a note to request it with the complete file.

A photocopy of a diary followed the letters. On the page for the day before his death, Graham had written: “P. Sayer — 6.30 p.m.”, then “confirm”. A line ran down towards a note for the following day. Nothing indicated whether it meant a call or a visit.

“He was expecting someone that evening,” Judith said.

“Paul?” Daniel asked.

“He didn't give me a name. I was at my sister's.”

She pulled a sleeve down over her wrist.

“He called me to ask where the biscuits were.”

Daniel looked at the small table. Graham knew where the bottles and drinking glasses were kept, and where Judith kept her medication. The biscuits remained a logistical problem. The memory made him hurt more than the diary had.

Ben asked whether he could photograph the page.

“You can take the copy. That's why it's there,” Judith said.

Paul had told them he had not seen Graham for months. A proposed appointment did not prove a meeting had happened. Daniel wrote the two facts side by side, without drawing an arrow.

Judith drew towards her a page Daniel had not yet read.

“This one, you can look at. I'm keeping it.”

It was a list of people to contact. Ruth was on it, with the note “follow up”. Beside Michael, Graham had written the name of a former firm of solicitors. The line was not ticked.

“Had he written to him?” Daniel asked.

“I don't know. He told me he needed to find an address.”

Ben leaned forward to read the list.

“For my mother, he had it.”

Judith nodded.

Daniel knew that need to put things in order before making contact. A page was missing, a name still needed checking: small tasks queued up ahead of the difficult letter. He thought of the question Helen Ward had passed on. What he was willing to write. He still had not replied.

“He meant well,” Daniel said.

Judith put the page down.

“He also wanted to finish his book. Both can be true.”

She took a thread from her shirt cuff.

“He'd chosen a photograph for the cover. He asked me whether it made him look old.”

“And?” Daniel asked.

“I told him the thirty years since had taken care of that.”

Daniel smiled. So did Judith, briefly. Then she put the list beneath a book. He did not ask to see it again.

Judith stood up to plug in the lamp. Daniel began to explain to her what he had replaced. At last, something he had known how to mend.

“Later,” she said.

The light illuminated a pile of crosswords. She moved the shade a few centimetres.

“There.”

At the back of the folder, Daniel found a page bearing a recent date. The same scene appeared there for a third time. In the first version, Graham had written: “Daniel Mercer's certainty allowed us to move forward.” The sentence had disappeared. In its place, there was a question: “Did we help her remember, or help her agree?” Daniel read the first version before putting both away. That was the one he would have preferred to take home.
