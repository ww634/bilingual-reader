# The Room Above the Yard

Jo Harris opened the door to them with a piece of adhesive tape stuck to her sleeve.

“You have twenty minutes,” Jo said. “At eleven, I have to explain to a customer why the glass costs more than her painting.”

“That often happens?” Ben asked.

“When the painting comes from her brother-in-law, yes.”

She let them in.

Mill Lane ran along the canal, east of the bus station. The building had three windows upstairs and an old delivery entrance at the side. Daniel had seen it in photographs at the time. He had never been inside. The file had given him the impression of having spent hours there. He knew the angles in the photographs, not the room.

They climbed an internal staircase that smelled of cut wood. At the top, two large tables occupied a bright room. Frames stood waiting against the wall, separated by pieces of cardboard. A radio was talking in another room. Jo switched it off.

“Anna's office was there,” Jo said.

She pointed to the table on which lay a watercolour of a boat. There was no mark on the floor or particular emptiness. Someone had painted the walls, laid flooring, paid the rent. The room had continued to be used.

Ben stopped beside a narrow strip in the ceiling.

“There used to be a partition here?”

“Yes. We removed it when I took over the upper floor. In 2013. Before that, I only had the ground floor,” Jo replied.

Ben followed a join with his eyes as far as the wall. He asked permission to photograph both ends.

“What does that tell you?” Daniel asked.

“That people did not move around the way we do. And that you are going to draw a room that is too big if you start now,” Ben replied.

Daniel had taken out his notebook. He kept it open.

“Then show me.”

Jo went to fetch a photograph of the building work on her computer. It showed the partition half dismantled, a door and an old radiator. She pointed out the position of the office where Ruth sometimes worked. It looked onto the yard, beside Anna's. The windows were closer together than the doors.

Ben bent down beside a cable trunking channel running along the skirting board. He did not touch it.

“This one runs in front of the old doorway. It was installed after it was closed up.”

Daniel followed the white line on the wall. He had taken it for part of the decor. Ben could read in it a sequence of jobs, as Daniel could in background noise.

“Can you say when?” Daniel asked.

“From this alone, no. But if someone gives you a plan with this socket and tells you it dates from 2001, you ask one more question,” Ben replied.

Jo confirmed that the wiring had been redone during the building work. She could send them the full photograph, with the other views from the same folder.

Daniel crossed out the word “plan” at the top of his drawing. He wrote “present layout”.

“Do you always have a pencil on you?” Ben asked.

“It works without a battery,” Daniel replied.

“So do I, before lunch.”

Daniel handed him the pencil. Ben added the old doorway with a dotted line.

Ben asked to see the yard. Jo led them to the rear stairs. The exit was still used for deliveries. At the bottom of the steps, pallets were stacked against a wall. Further along, a passage led to the street. It was an ordinary entrance, visible from the windows.

Daniel asked Jo to speak from the upper door.

“What shall I say?”

“Anything,” Daniel replied.

“The glass costs more than the painting.”

Ben lowered his head. Daniel heard his laugh before seeing him smile.

Jo's voice reached him clearly. When she stepped back into the room, he lost the end of her sentence. He noted down their positions, the open door and the closed windows. Beside these conditions, he noted what would need checking against the old plans.

Back upstairs, he explained the possible path of the sound. Two separate offices could communicate through open windows onto the same yard. Sound did not need to pass through the partition. But the glazing, the doors, the furniture had changed. They did not know where each person had stood, or what had been open.

“So she could hear without understanding everything,” Ben said.

“Yes. That is a possibility,” Daniel replied.

Jo looked at the worktable.

“Anna used to talk a lot to herself. It took me a while to realise that she was recording. She had a dictation recorder. Invoice references, things to check. She said she could talk faster than she could write.”

“Did you know her well?” Daniel asked.

“Well enough to lend her a stapler and have to go and get it back,” Jo replied.

Jo picked up the tape that had come off her sleeve.

“She wanted to leave. Find work in Birmingham. One day, she asked me whether a plant would survive a move by bus. I offered her our van. She said she was only talking about the plant.”

She smiled, then looked at the time.

“Did you have a business here with your husband?” Ben asked.

He was looking at an old sign in the photograph of the building work. Harris Maintenance.

“On the ground floor, yes. I started framing after we closed. Martin sold the equipment. As for me, I kept the premises,” Jo said.

“Did you work for Sayer?” Daniel asked.

“For the buildings he managed. Then there was an issue with payments. Invoices that were said to have been paid and which Martin was still chasing,” Jo replied.

Daniel stopped drawing.

“Before Anna died?”

“It had started before. It ended long afterwards,” Jo replied.

There had been a dispute, lawyers, an agreement which Jo did not want to summarise the terms of. Martin had kept a file for that matter. He had taken it when they separated. She could pass on a request to him, not promise them his papers.

“He keeps everything,” Jo said. “I cannot guarantee you that he can find everything.”

The bell rang downstairs. The customer was early. Daniel left his contact details and those of the firm he had contacted. On the way down, Ben let through a woman carrying a painting wrapped in a blanket. He waited until he was in the street to look at Daniel.

“Do you think she took the cheapest glass?”

Daniel smiled despite himself.

His phone vibrated before he replied. The firm had received his request. Michael's solicitor, Helen Ward, could speak to him at half past eleven. He confirmed the telephone appointment. In his notebook, beside the drawing of the yard, he wrote “complete recording — source?”
