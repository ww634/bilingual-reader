# Before the Trial

At one o'clock, Ben opened the flat door and called for his mother. He stopped halfway through the word “Mum”. The police officer accompanying them said nothing. Daniel wiped his shoes longer than necessary.

Ruth lived on the first floor of a small house divided in two, ten minutes from the canal. It was not the house where Daniel had met her. In the hallway, an umbrella was drying in a basin. On the wall, a calendar showed a dog that seemed to regret its pumpkin costume.

“She buys it every year,” Ben said. “She claims it is for me.”

The officer reminded them of what had been agreed. They would look at the blue folder that Ruth had offered her son to read. The other papers would stay where they were. Useful documents would be photographed before any decision about keeping them. Ben pointed to the sitting-room table. He did not open the drawers as he passed.

The folder was under a gardening magazine. Ruth had three pots on a balcony barely wider than the table. The magazine promised an orchard. It must be counting on the neighbours' cooperation. Daniel took the chair indicated to him and waited for the officer to finish her photographs.

Inside, the sheets were not arranged by date. There were copies of letters, newspaper cuttings, a pay envelope bearing the name Sayer Property Services. Correspondence about invoices had been stapled behind a timesheet. They did not separate them.

“Was she an accountant?” Daniel asked.

“She kept the books. She used to say that it was different,” Ben replied.

Ben touched the edge of the envelope, without moving it.

“She worked in the evenings, when I was asleep. Well, when she thought I was asleep. She had a calculator with a paper roll. I used to take the roll to make roads.”

“An expensive use.”

“She thought so too.”

Ben asked the officer whether he could water the plants. She said yes. He found a glass in the kitchen and filled it twice. Daniel watched him remove the dry leaves from a pot of basil. He kept them in his palm so as not to dirty the floor.

“She gave me a plant when I got my flat,” Ben said. “She used to come to see how it was doing.”

“The plant?” Daniel asked.

“Officially.”

He shook the leaves over the bin. The officer received a call and stepped away towards the hall. Ben followed her movement until she indicated to him that it was not news about Ruth. He put down the glass carefully.

Daniel recognised that way of watching a telephone, as though one had to help it ring. At the hospital, he had watched the pockets of people coming in. He did not mention it. Ben had returned to the table and was showing him a date. For now, that was what they could do.

The letter was behind a cutting dated the year of the trial. It was a copy addressed to Graham, on 22 October 2001. Daniel read the first paragraph, then read it again.

“I would like to make clear what I said. I did not see his face for long enough to be certain. I thought of Michael, but I already knew they had argued. I do not want this to become something I am supposed to have seen clearly.”

Ben leaned towards the sheet.

“That is what she was saying last week.”

Daniel looked for an annotation at the bottom of the copy. Nothing showed whether the original had been sent, or when Graham had received it. He said so. Ben let him finish.

“But she wrote it.”

“Yes.”

The next paragraph described the yard on Mill Lane. A door had slammed. Ruth had looked through the service window. The man was walking away, something flat against his side. “A stiff document folder, or a file. I could see a right angle under his arm. I did not see what was inside it.” The letter ended with a request not to be called at work.

Daniel looked at the pay envelope.

“Paul Sayer is still here?” Daniel asked.

“He has an office near the town hall. I called him last night. He had not heard anything. He told me to come by if I wanted,” Ben replied.

Daniel remembered the name, not the man. It belonged to the part of the case that had taken place without him.

The newspaper cutting reported that Ruth had recognised Michael leaving the scene. It summarised a day in court in a few columns. Daniel placed it beside the letter, noting their original positions in the folder.

“A newspaper article is not a trial transcript,” Ben said.

“No.”

“You were going to tell me that.”

“Probably.”

The officer photographed both documents for Shah. Daniel explained that they would need to find the accounts from the time. Ruth's memory could have become clearer. She could also have used different words depending on the question. He heard himself lining up possibilities like chairs in front of a door. He did not add the third.

A paper sleeve slipped between two sheets. It was empty. On the front, Ruth had written “cassette — home”.

“She used to record things,” Ben said. “Lists. What she needed to ask the doctor. I was not allowed to touch the little machine.”

“Do you know what was on this one?” Daniel asked.

Ben shook his head.

An empty sleeve could have been empty since yesterday or for twenty years. They left it in the folder. The officer noted down the inscription. Daniel looked at Ben's hands. He had folded the edge of his sandwich bag several times. He still had not eaten it.

Before leaving, they agreed to arrange a request for the old trial papers through an appropriate channel. A recent article, found by Ben, gave the firm still handling Michael's legal efforts. Daniel sent a request for contact, specifying his former role. He had neither a new report nor a conclusion to offer.

Ben found the final note tucked into the manuscript envelope. Ruth had written “Graham wants me to confirm”. Below it, in quotation marks: “The first name was sufficiently distinct.” Daniel knew that wording. He had written it in the margin of his old transcript. The letter in which Ruth said she was not certain was still open in front of him.
