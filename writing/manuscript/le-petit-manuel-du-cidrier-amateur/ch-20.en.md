# The Next Harvest

The notebook opens at a page where a small stain has dried. You may no longer know whether it came from juice or coffee. The notes, fortunately, are easier to identify. They connect the first apple observed to the questions raised by the glass.

We began by looking at what the apple could contribute. We distinguished sweetness, acidity, bitterness and astringency. Then we followed the trees, the harvest and the actions of processing. This journey provides reference points without turning every orchard into an identical exercise.

Our main example chose a more accessible route for beginning. It starts with purchased pasteurised juice without fermentation-inhibiting preservatives. The reference strain remained SafCider AB-1. The plan remained a dry, still cider.

The ten litres were a starting quantity. We have not measured an actual final yield throughout these pages. Nor have we observed an actual fermentation whose success we could announce. The journey illustrates decisions to make, not a harvest of invented results.

In your own notebook, begin by separating facts and impressions. A date, a volume or an instrument reading belongs among recorded observations. A sensation of freshness is a sensory observation to specify with its conditions. A possible explanation remains a hypothesis until it is supported.

Suppose you are writing the review of an imaginary personal trial. You might record that you prepared the equipment before opening the juice. You might describe the temperature that varied during the night. Then specify what you would change to monitor that variation better.

These sentences tell three different things. What you did, the conditions that existed and what you are considering next. The perfect tense, the imperfect and the conditional thus find their place naturally. Your French teacher could almost be hiding behind the apple tree.

To prepare the next trial, choose a sufficiently precise question. Ask, for example, how two identified juices differ before fermentation. Or investigate whether your measurement method gives sufficiently repeatable readings. “Doing better” is a generous intention but still too broad a question.

Change one element at a time when you wish to understand its effect. Keep the other conditions comparable as far as possible. If several things change, record them rather than forgetting them. Real life rarely agrees to become a perfectly obedient laboratory.

A useful trial must remain compatible with controlled production. You do not test a bottle's resistance by letting an uncertain fermentation continue inside it. You do not bypass a product's instructions to see what will happen. Curiosity works better with understood limits than with dangerous surprises.

If you have trees, return to observe them before the next harvest. Record flowering, weather conditions and differences between varieties. Watch how the fruit develops on the branches. The orchard does not start working on the day you take out the baskets.

For horticultural work, return to guidance suited to your region and trees. Rootstock, variety and climate remain important. A technique heard of elsewhere sometimes requires local adaptation. The neighbour's apple tree does not necessarily read the same weather forecast as your book.

If you buy your juice, your learning remains just as practical. Keep information about its origin, treatment and ingredients. Compare batches using measurements and consistent descriptions. The absence of a personal orchard does not prevent serious attention to apples.

Before starting again, inspect the equipment and fill in the missing information. Find the instructions for the instruments, products and yeast. Check their current guidance rather than copying a dose that has become automatic. A habit benefits from remaining a conscious decision.

Share your questions with experienced people while bringing precise information. Describe the conditions, the observations and what you have already checked. Listen to different methods while asking which products they apply to. An interesting tradition becomes more useful when its conditions are understood.

One day, an apple will seem less anonymous to you than before. You will think of its tree, its sugars, its tannins and the juice's possible journey. You will still have questions with more words to ask them. That will be an excellent reason to reopen the notebook.
