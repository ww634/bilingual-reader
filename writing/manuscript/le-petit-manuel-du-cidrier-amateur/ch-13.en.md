# Racking Without Rushing

At the bottom of the vessel, a deposit takes shape like a miniature landscape. The cider above may look clearer. Your hose waits in its storage place, full of silent promises. Before taking it out, let us define the work it is meant to do.

Racking means transferring the liquid while leaving a deposit behind. This deposit is often called the lees. It contains, among other things, yeast and particles from the juice. All this yeast is not necessarily dead.

A distinction is made between coarser lees and finer lees. Their composition depends on the juice and the work already done. A deposit of fruit particles does not always behave like a mainly yeasty deposit. Visible thickness is therefore not enough to decide the timing alone.

Racking can help to separate cider from unwanted lees. It can also prepare for a planned step in a maturation protocol. But every transfer adds handling, losses and possible exposure to oxygen. The right question is its usefulness, not the number of vessels available.

Our imaginary batch is still intended to become a dry, still cider. We assign it no precise date for compulsory racking. The measured development, the condition of the lees and the chosen process guide the decision. A fermentation in difficulty first requires the assessment from the previous chapter.

Prepare the receiving vessel before moving the one containing the cider. Choose materials suitable for food contact with an acidic, alcoholic drink. Check the hoses, the seals and the taps. One small forgotten seal may have a great ambition to leak.

Clean, then sanitise every surface in contact with the cider. Follow the product conditions and keep the hose ends clean. A sanitised hose loses that advantage if it then trails on the floor. Also prepare a stable position for each vessel.

If the transfer uses gravity, the source vessel must be higher. Position it securely, without unstable stacking or dangerous effort. Recent movement can put the lees back into suspension. Allow the deposit to settle again as the process requires, rather than starting in a cloud.

Use a priming device suitable for cider and for your equipment. Do not start the siphon with your mouth, even if your great-uncle did. Family memories have their place, but not in the hose. Follow the instructions to avoid introducing contaminants at the start.

Position the inlet above the lees, without drawing up the bottom layer. Keep the outlet near the bottom of the receiving vessel at the start of the transfer. Then keep it below the surface to limit splashing. A gentle flow is better than a small decorative waterfall.

Watch the level and the position of both ends. Avoid letting the hose draw in air. Stop before the sediment is drawn through in quantity. Accept leaving a little cider with the lees.

Seeking the last drop can compromise the purpose of racking. Useful yield is not simply the volume moved. It is also the quality retained during this operation. Your patience may be more rewarding than a spectacular tilt.

After the transfer, the space above the cider deserves your attention. During active fermentation, room must be allowed for foam and gas release. For later storage, a large volume of air can encourage oxidation and some spoilage. Choose a vessel suited to the stage and to the volume actually remaining.

The carbon dioxide present does not form a permanent magic lid. Gases can mix, and openings change the situation. Do not add juice solely to fill the empty space without reviewing the process. That addition brings, among other things, sugars that can still ferment.

Measure the recovered volume and record what you left behind. Our starting ten litres are not ten guaranteed litres at the end. Samples and racking gradually reduce the available quantity. We await actual measurements before calculating any treatment for this volume.

Finally, restore the closure and the appropriate gas management. Racking does not sterilise cider. It guarantees neither that yeast has stopped nor the preservation of residual sweetness. Its role is more modest: to separate carefully, then let the next stage proceed properly.
