# Enter the Yeast

The juice is in its vessel. The thermometer has found its place, and your notebook still bears no sticky mark. Let us use this brief pause to introduce yeast. They will do much of the work, but it is up to you to prepare the conditions.

A yeast is a living organism, consisting of a single cell. During alcoholic fermentation, it converts sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Its activity also contributes to the aromatic profile of the drink. Yeast therefore does more than make sweetness disappear.

A strain is a lineage with particular characteristics. Two strains may belong to the same species without behaving in exactly the same way. A selected yeast is chosen for properties that are known under certain conditions. The packet gives you a documented starting point, not a guaranteed result.

A spontaneous fermentation relies on populations already present in the juice and its surroundings. It can produce interesting ciders, but requires knowledge of the process and its variability. The word natural is not enough to describe a method or to guarantee its result. For our first batch, we keep the route of a selected yeast.

Let us take a specific commercial example: SafCider AB-1, from Fermentis. This choice illustrates how to read a technical sheet; other yeasts may be suitable. The manufacturer's page, consulted in October 2026, states twenty to thirty grams per hectolitre for primary fermentation. A hectolitre corresponds to a hundred litres.

For ten litres, that range therefore becomes two to three grams. An illustrative dose of two and a half grams falls within that range. This calculation concerns this product and this volume, not all yeasts. Always check the current instructions for the product used before weighing.

There are older documents whose recommendations differ. The number most often copied on the internet is not necessarily the most recent. Record the exact name, the batch number and the consultation date. This precision will allow you to understand a choice later, even if the packaging has disappeared.

Temperature is part of the choice. For this product, the manufacturer states a fermentation range of ten to thirty degrees Celsius. That broad range does not mean that every point produces the same pace or the same aromas. For our example, let us choose a working temperature around twenty degrees, without presenting it as a universal optimum.

Measure the juice temperature, not just the room temperature. Fermentation activity produces heat, and the juice temperature may differ from the surrounding air temperature. Choose a place where you can monitor variations, out of direct sunlight and without sudden changes. A radiator is not a fermentation controller simply because it heats.

Yeast also need nutrients. Sugar provides energy, but does not constitute a complete diet on its own. Yeast-assimilable nitrogen refers to the forms of nitrogen that yeast can use. Its amount varies according to the juice, and requirements also depend on the strain and conditions.

A yeast described as undemanding still has nutritional requirements. To decide on an addition, use a relevant analysis and the recommendations suited to the juice and product. A laboratory or a qualified adviser can help you to establish that plan. We will not set a nutrient dose without that information and we will not replace a suitable product with a kitchen ingredient.

Inoculation is the introduction of the chosen yeast into the juice. For AB-1, the current method allows direct addition to the surface of the must, with gentle mixing and homogenisation according to the technical sheet. Must here means the juice intended for fermentation. Another procedure provides for prior rehydration; do not mix instructions taken from several methods.

Before this step, check equipment preparation, the actual volume, the temperature and the nutrition plan. The calculated dose must correspond to the volume actually inoculated, after sampling. The added yeast does not sanitise a questionable juice. Keep the vessel protected, with a gas outlet that is suitable and never blocked.

The packet's condition deserves a check before opening. Consult the stated date and the storage conditions specified by the manufacturer. A damaged or poorly stored packet is not a good starting point. If you use only part of its contents, follow the specific instructions for the remainder, instead of leaving it on a shelf.

After inoculation, record the time, the amount added and the observed temperature. There may be a delay before visible signs of activity. That delay does not justify multiplying additions or heating the batch on its own. An observation should lead to a check before becoming an intervention.

Our imaginary batch now has a reference yeast and a working temperature. The numbers in this example remain teaching choices, not observations from an actual fermentation. In the next chapter, we will learn to monitor what happens, using measurements and patience. The airlock may have something to say; it will not say everything.
