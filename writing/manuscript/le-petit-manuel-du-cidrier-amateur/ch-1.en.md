# One Apple, Many Possibilities

An apple fits in your hand. An entire orchard sometimes fits into a smell. You open a crate, and there it is, a memory: damp grass, a fresh skin, a cut fruit. Before the hoses and the bottles, cider begins with this attention.

In this book, the word cider means a drink made through the fermentation of apple juice. Yeasts convert sugars into alcohol and into carbon dioxide. They are microscopic organisms, not tiny workers with a schedule. We will learn to monitor their activity rather than give them a deadline.

This transformation changes the taste of the juice. A very sweet juice will not necessarily produce a very sweet cider. Part of the pleasure lies in understanding what remains, what disappears and what emerges. We do not simply preserve an apple in a bottle: we guide a change.

To begin, let us imagine a first batch of ten litres of juice. This is an example that we will follow throughout the book. Our goal will be a dry, still cider. Dry here means low in sweetness; still means without deliberate carbonation. The cider therefore does not need to bubble to have something to say.

We will choose a selected yeast for this example. Its precise use will come in the chapter devoted to fermentation. The ten litres refer to the starting volume, not to a promise of ten litres in bottles. Samples and transfers will reduce that volume. Allowing for these small differences is already working carefully.

The journey begins in the orchard, where the apple trees produce our raw material. We will look at the varieties, the trees and the seasons. Then we will learn to harvest and to sort the fruit. Milling breaks them down into pieces; pressing allows us to extract the juice.

Next, we will need to understand this juice, prepare fermentation and observe its development. Maturation, any blending and bottling will have their place. Each stage answers a different question. Which apple should we choose? What does this instrument measure? Is the cider ready? A good question is often worth more than an action performed out of habit.

You do not have an orchard? You can still follow this path. Buying apples from a grower allows you to begin without planting a tree. A suitable juice also provides a starting point, provided you check its composition and treatment. We will return to this choice during preparation. Your address does not need to end with “farm”.

You can also read without making cider. Recognising a flavour, understanding a label or asking a better question are already pleasures. At the market, asking when an apple was picked opens a conversation. Asking why it suits cider can extend it until closing time.

Keep a notebook, if you feel like it. A date, a variety name and a few precise words are enough to begin. Write “this apple makes my mouth water” rather than “good”. The first comment describes a sensation; the second expresses a judgement. Both are useful, but they do not say the same thing.

This distinction will stay with us everywhere. Seeing a deposit is not the same thing as understanding its origin. Smelling a pleasant aroma is not enough to establish that a product is safe. We will use our senses to describe, and appropriate methods to check.

There will be new words. A rootstock, tannins, a racking operation: these terms seem serious, but they describe concrete things. We will encounter them when they are useful, with an explanation and an example. Nobody will ask you to recite a dictionary before touching an apple.

Now imagine two friends looking at the same juice. The first announces that he wants a sweet, sparkling cider. The second prefers a dry cider to accompany a meal. They start with the same fruit, but their decisions will not be identical. Their first task is to define what they are looking for, not to copy the last piece of advice they heard.

Sweetness and bubbles are two distinct characteristics. A cider can be dry and sparkling, or sweet and still. However, achieving the desired result requires a coherent method. We will explain the other possibilities without changing our first goal at every discovery.

This way of working will also help you to learn French. A word becomes easier to remember when it answers a real question. You will encounter “to choose” beside a tree, then beside a vessel. You will encounter “to compare” again when tasting a fruit and then when taking a measurement. Vocabulary will make several journeys, and you with it.

One final clarification before setting off: the first chapters prepare the later ones. Do not put juice into a sealed bottle to try fermentation based only on this introduction. The gas produced must be managed with appropriate equipment. We will take the time to explain each stage before carrying it out.

There remains one rule for reading that is very simple: do not look for a universal recipe behind every sentence. A tree grows in a particular place; a juice has its own characteristics. Learning to make cider means learning to recognise these differences and to choose accordingly. For now, let us leave the bottle on the table. Let us take an apple and the time to get to know it.
