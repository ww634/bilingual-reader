# A Year in the Orchard

In winter, a leafless apple tree resembles a sentence whose construction becomes visible. The trunk divides, the branches cross, some shoots occupy the same space. Before taking out the secateurs, take a walk around the tree. Your first tool will be your eye.

The seasons described here correspond to a temperate climate in the Northern Hemisphere. Exact dates vary by region and year. Observe the development of the tree and follow local advice rather than a rigid calendar. A bud does not read your diary, even if you have organised it very well.

For a mature apple tree grown as a free-standing tree, maintenance pruning is generally carried out in winter, during dormancy. Trained forms have different needs, particularly for summer pruning. You therefore need to identify the form and the age of the tree before applying a method.

Pruning aims to organise the branch structure, to let in light and to preserve fruiting wood. This last term describes the wood that bears the structures capable of producing fruit. We are not trying to give the same haircut to every tree. Shortening all the tips without thinking can remove future fruit.

Severe pruning can cause strong regrowth. That is not a reason to cut more the following year. For a neglected tree, plan a gradual restoration with appropriate advice. If you are unsure about a large branch, keep the saw idle while you seek information. A cut branch cannot be glued back with good intentions.

In spring, the buds open and flowering transforms the orchard. Take the time to notice which trees flower together. Your choice of partners for pollination becomes a visible reality. Record the beginning and the end of flowering, without claiming to predict the whole harvest.

Many flowers do not guarantee many apples. Frost can damage the flowers, and pollination depends on several conditions. Fruit set is the beginning of the development of young fruit after flowering. It is a useful word for that moment when the promise begins to take shape.

In early summer, some of the small fruit may fall naturally. This drop does not necessarily signal a catastrophe. Look at what remains on the branches before worrying. A tree may still carry too much fruit after this drop.

Thinning means removing some of the young fruit when the crop load is too heavy. It can improve their development and reduce the risk of broken branches. The amount to remove depends on the tree, on its vigour and on the production goal. Do not transfer a rule for large dessert apples automatically to all cider apples.

Some trees produce heavily one year, then very little the next. We speak of biennial bearing. Appropriate management of the crop load can help to limit this phenomenon. Your notebook takes on here a new value: it allows you to compare several years. A lightly laden tree this year does not tell its whole story.

In summer, monitor the available water, especially for young trees. A mulch can help to retain soil moisture and to limit competing weeds. Keep it away from the trunk. Also check ties and stakes: the tree grows, and an over-tight tie can injure it.

During your visits, look at the leaves, the fruit and the bark. A mark or a damaged leaf is not enough to identify a disease. Photograph the problem, record its progression and seek an appropriate diagnosis if necessary. A treatment chosen at random is not a form of care.

Choose an observation point that you will find again easily. A photograph taken from that same spot at several moments can help you to see what memory confuses. Add a brief note about the weather and about what has changed. This is not a matter of monitoring every leaf like a guard. It is a matter of building knowledge of your tree.

Suppose that a branch carries little fruit. Instead of writing “bad branch”, describe what you know. Did you observe flowers there? Did you prune this part the previous winter? Is the branch in shade for much of the day? These questions do not give the answer automatically, but they guide your investigation.

For the cider maker, this patience has a very practical value. It allows you to discuss the harvest differently than by counting crates. You can describe the conditions in which the fruit developed. The juice no longer begins at the press: it belongs to a season that you have followed.

Even without an orchard, you can follow this year through conversations with your supplier. Ask them what surprised them during the season. Listen to the answer before talking about quantities that you want to buy. You will learn words and realities that the price per kilogram does not convey.

As autumn approaches, your visits revolve around a new question. It is no longer only a matter of seeing whether the apples are growing, but of understanding whether they are ripening. Their colour attracts the eye; their development requires several observations. After a year spent watching the tree, you will be better prepared to choose the right moment. The harvest is approaching, but the basket can still wait a little.
