# Dry, Sweet, Still or Sparkling?

Faced with a glass, people often ask whether the cider is sweet. They also ask whether it sparkles, sometimes before even smelling it. These questions concern two different dimensions. To choose a style, it is better to examine them separately.

Sweetness concerns, among other things, the sugars remaining in the drink. Sparkling character concerns dissolved carbon dioxide and its expression when served. A dry cider can therefore be sparkling. A sweet cider can also be still.

Let us add a third question, less visible in the glass. Will the product remain in its intended state during storage? This stability cannot be inferred from the pleasure of the first sip. Taste, bubbles and stability are not interchangeable.

In our project, the chosen route remains dry and still. We do not add a sweetening step to make the ending more festive. Nor do we declare fermentation complete because this choice seems simple. Checks before bottling will be revisited in a dedicated chapter.

A dry cider is not an apple deprived of all aroma. Fruity notes may persist without substantial sweetness. An impression of ripe pear therefore does not demonstrate the presence of fermentable sugar. The nose offers images, while analyses answer other questions.

Our perception of sweetness also depends on the overall balance. Acidity, bitterness and astringency can alter that impression. Two ciders with a comparable sugar content may seem different. That is why a sensory description usefully complements a measurement.

Here, the words “dry” and “sweet” describe stylistic intentions. They do not constitute a regulatory scale valid everywhere. For sale or labelling, the applicable definitions must be checked. Our production notebook and a commercial label do not have the same function.

To obtain sweetness, some processes retain some of the juice sugars. Others involve an addition after fermentation. Both routes require appropriate control of stability. Retaining sugars does not mean having removed the ability to ferment.

Added apple juice or honey can feed the remaining yeast. Even a pasteurised ingredient does not sterilise the cider into which it is mixed. Refermentation may then produce gas and alter the taste. In closed packaging, that gas can cause dangerous pressure.

The stability of a sweet cider therefore requires a complete, verified process. Professionals may combine analytical checks and different means of microbiological control. The choice depends on the product, the equipment and the actual conditions. An isolated operation does not replace that whole system.

Sorbate, for example, inhibits yeast multiplication. It does not kill all yeast and is not a reliable way to stop active fermentation. Sulphite effectiveness depends, among other things, on pH and product conditions. These products must not become imaginary buttons marked “finished”.

Coarse filtration may improve appearance without providing sufficient microbiological control. Filtration targeting microorganisms requires suitable equipment, a protocol and checks. Cold slows activity but does not guarantee stability after warming. To explore these methods, seek training and a complete procedure suitable for cider.

Sparkling cider can obtain its gas in several ways. Forced carbonation introduces carbon dioxide with equipment designed for pressure. Bottle fermentation produces gas through yeast activity. These methods do not require the same actions or checks.

In both cases, pressure must be controlled. Vessels, closures and equipment must be suited to the process. A decorative bottle does not acquire a known resistance thanks to its pretty stopper. We offer here neither a universal sugar dose nor improvised heating of sealed bottles.

One particular tradition finally deserves a brief detour. Clarification of the must, often called “keeving” in English, involves pectins. A brown cap may form, carrying away some nutrients and yeast. This separation may allow a slower fermentation.

It is a demanding, variable technique, not a shortcut to a risk-free sweet bottle. Our purchased pasteurised juice does not automatically become a candidate for this traditional process. So let us keep to our route while better understanding the neighbouring paths. We can then explore cider blending, with taste and stability still in the same notebook.
