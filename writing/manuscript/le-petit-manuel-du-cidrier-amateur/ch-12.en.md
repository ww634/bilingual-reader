# When Cider Raises a Question

One morning, the airlock stays silent and your imagination becomes very active. In the evening, an unexpected smell makes you wrinkle your nose. These moments are part of learning. The important thing is to separate what you observe from what you suppose.

An observation describes an accessible fact, with its conditions. You write, for example, that specific gravity remains identical across two comparable readings. A hypothesis proposes a possible explanation, without declaring it certain. The next action must help to distinguish the explanations.

Let us first consider the silence of the airlock after inoculation. It may be that the gas is escaping through another opening. It may also be that the yeast is still in a starting phase. These possibilities do not prove that all is well.

Check the closure and the gas vent without blocking the latter. Compare the temperature with the guidance for the yeast used. Reread the preparation notes and the ingredients of the purchased juice. A reliable specific gravity measurement tells you more than a contest of worried looks.

Our example retains the same pasteurised juice without fermentation-inhibiting preservatives. But an actual batch requires checking its own label. The memory of a package sometimes resembles a very loose translation. So keep a photograph or a copy of the useful information.

If specific gravity does not fall, first check the quality of the readings. The hydrometer may be touching the bottom of the measuring cylinder. Clinging bubbles or an inadequately accounted-for temperature can also distort the comparison. A well-prepared second measurement is worth more than correcting an imaginary problem.

A refractometer used after fermentation begins requires appropriate interpretation. Alcohol changes its reading, as we have already explained. A raw value cannot be compared directly with that of the initial juice. So change your interpretation before changing the entire contents of the vessel.

A confirmed standstill before the expected result requires a search for the cause. Temperature, nutrition and yeast preparation can play a part. The juice itself may present other difficulties. None of these possibilities justifies an automatic nutrient addition.

Adding more yeast is not always a sufficient solution. Restarting fermentation may require a specific protocol and analyses. Ask for advice with your measurements, rather than with the word “stuck” alone. A specialist cannot examine a vessel by telepathy.

Let us now turn to a smell reminiscent of rotten eggs. It may suggest sulphur compounds, including hydrogen sulphide. Several factors may contribute to their appearance. The smell alone reveals neither the exact cause nor the appropriate treatment.

Avoid remedies based on coins or copper pipes. Copper can alter aromas and cause other problems. Improvised aeration can also make some situations worse. Seek a documented diagnosis, not a trick that shines at the bottom of the drawer.

An unknown film or a fuzzy growth calls for a different kind of caution. Isolate the suspect batch and avoid contaminating equipment for other batches. Do not taste to decide whether the product is safe. A lovely colour and a pleasant aroma are no more proof.

An uncertain identification must not become a certainty thanks to a reassuring photograph. Have the problem assessed by a competent person if a decision remains possible. When a safety concern cannot be resolved, exclude the batch from consumption. Recovering a few litres is not worth a risky experiment.

To ask for useful help, prepare a short history of the batch concerned. State the juice, the strain and the inoculation conditions. Add the temperatures, the dated measurements and all products already used. Specify the known quantities and state frankly which ones are missing.

When an intervention is justified, record its objective before carrying it out. Follow the chosen protocol and plan how to check its effect. Changing everything at once often makes the result impossible to interpret. The notebook also helps you recognise that a hypothesis was wrong.

An attentive cider maker does not have an answer to every question. They learn to make their questions more precise. This skill will soon help you with the sediment at the bottom of the vessel. Before taking out a hose, you will need to ask why the cider should be transferred.
