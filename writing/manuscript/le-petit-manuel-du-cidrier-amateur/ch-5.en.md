# The Right Moment to Harvest

The basket is ready. So are you. Are the apples ready too? A lovely red colour makes you want to begin, but it does not constitute an official appointment with ripeness. To harvest at the right time, you need several clues.

Begin by finding out the usual period for harvesting your variety in your region. This period tells you when to observe more closely. It does not replace observation. The year's weather and orchard conditions can shift the calendar. The grower who sold you the tree can give you a reference point, not a departure time.

Choose several fruits that are representative of the tree. Do not sample only the largest apple on the sunny side. Look at fruit from several branches, in different positions that are safely accessible. Your sample should tell you about the crop, not just about its star pupil.

Observe the background colour of the skin, beneath any red areas. For some varieties, its change provides a useful clue. Firmness, flavour and ease of detachment also provide information. None of these signs should become a single rule for every apple.

To test detachment, gently take the fruit in your hand, lift it and turn it slightly. A ready apple often comes away easily, but varieties differ. Do not turn this test into a test of strength. If you have to pull roughly, stop and reconsider the situation.

Using sound fruit that has been washed before tasting, compare the flavour with your earlier notes. Has the apple become more aromatic? Has its balance changed? Seed colour may provide an additional indication, but brown seeds do not certify ideal ripeness on their own. Bring together the clues instead of asking a single one to decide for all of them.

Professionals can supplement these observations with measurements and maturity tests. An iodine test allows, for example, observation of the distribution of starch in the flesh. Its interpretation requires a method and appropriate reference material. This is not an improvised recipe to try with the contents of a medicine cabinet. Fruit used for an iodine test is not intended to be eaten.

When you harvest, place the apples in clean containers rather than throwing them. An apple destined for the press deserves careful handling. The fact that it will be milled later does not justify damaging it during transport. Separate the varieties and record the harvest dates on the crates.

Sorting begins at harvest and continues before pressing. Discard rotten or mouldy fruit. Do not taste them to decide whether they are usable. Some moulds can produce an undesirable substance called patulin. For the whole-apple route, select sound fruit, without relying on fermentation to correct poor sorting.

Windfall apples require particular attention. A fall can cause damage, and contact with the ground can introduce contamination. For the beginner's route in this book, prefer fruit picked from the tree. Reject fruit soiled with animal droppings or whose collection conditions are uncertain. A clean appearance does not guarantee the absence of unwanted microbes.

It is sometimes necessary to store the apples before pressing them. Choose a cool place, clean and protected from animals, with conditions suited to the fruit. Keep the batches identifiable and inspect them regularly. Remove fruit that is deteriorating. The possible storage time depends on the variety, the condition of the fruit and the storage conditions.

You may hear of resting apples before pressing. Do not conclude from this that all apples benefit from waiting the same number of days. Ripening and rotting are not two equivalent ways of becoming softer. Without an appropriate method for your fruit, do not improvise a long wait.

To compare two visits to the orchard, record what you actually observed. “More aromatic than on the last visit” is information. “It is time because I am free on Sunday” is a wish. Your availability obviously matters, but it does not change the condition of the fruit. It is better to organise the work around the harvest than to ask for the reverse.

If you buy the fruit, ask the picking date and the storage conditions. Examine several apples, not just those placed on top. A batch should be judged as a whole. Agree on transport and on the time when you can deal with the fruit. Apples do not become less fragile because you bought them at a good price.

Finally keep a record of what you discard. If one crate contains many damaged fruits, look for what may have happened during harvesting or transport. Sorting protects the current batch and teaches you something for the next. It is not wasted time: it is the beginning of more precise work.

For this whole-apple route, we have a selection principle, not yet a guaranteed weight of fruit. We will measure the juice obtained rather than promise an identical yield for every basket. The crates are ready, the fruit is identified, and sorting has already begun. The next stage will be to prepare a workspace as carefully as we have chosen our apples.
