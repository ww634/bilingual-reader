# Following Fermentation

The vessel seems motionless on its shelf. Inside, however, the juice may be changing profoundly. Following fermentation means learning to read these changes without inventing them. Your notebook becomes a quiet companion, less talkative than the airlock.

Let us return to our example of purchased pasteurised juice, intended to become dry and still. The chosen yeast is still SafCider AB-1. We are describing a possible approach, not an actually observed fermentation. No date yet allows us to announce the end of the work.

Begin each visit by looking at the vessel without opening it. Check its position, its condition and the gas outlet device. The airlock must operate according to its instructions, with the specified liquid level. Abundant foam must not be able to block this outlet.

During fermentation, carbon dioxide is released. An ordinary vessel does not become a pressure tank through goodwill. Keep an appropriate vent, without an improvised airtight closure. Work in a ventilated space, because the gas can accumulate in a confined place.

The first bubbles are often a small domestic event. One almost feels like giving them first names. Above all, resist the urge to count each bubble as a gram of sugar gone. The airlock indicates a passage of gas, not a measured quantity of sugar.

A leak at the lid can make the airlock silent. A change in temperature can also alter the release of gas. Gas already dissolved can escape without substantial new production. Numerous or absent bubbles therefore require careful interpretation.

Record the temperature in the same place and with the same instrument. Distinguish the room temperature from that of the liquid if your equipment allows this measurement. Fermentation produces heat, and the two temperatures may differ. An isolated reading tells less than a series of comparable measurements.

In our example, surroundings around twenty degrees remain a working reference. This figure does not become an ideal temperature for every yeast. Consult the current guidance for the strain and avoid sudden changes. The radiator is not a button for speeding up the calendar.

To follow sugars indirectly, let us return to specific gravity. Plan useful samples, rather than an opening whenever you pass through the room. Clean and sanitise the sampling equipment according to the protocol already chosen. Then close the vessel properly, while retaining its gas vent.

Prepare the sample for a reliable reading with the hydrometer. Remove the bubbles clinging to the instrument, without shaking the whole batch. Follow the reference temperature and the reading method for your model. The used sample does not go back into the main vessel.

Let us now imagine an independent series to understand a trend. Comparable readings go from 1.050 to 1.030, then to 1.014. Specific gravity decreases, which indicates progress in this example. These numbers are neither our batch results nor a programme to reproduce.

If the decrease slows, first describe what you have measured. A fermentation may become slower for several reasons. Stable readings may correspond to completion or to a premature stop. They must be examined alongside the batch history, rather than alone becoming permission to bottle.

The nose brings another kind of information. A fresh apple note may give way to a more yeasty impression. Simply describe what strikes you, without immediately seeking a scholarly name. A pleasant smell does not prove, however, that the product is safe.

In the notebook, associate each observation with a date and time. Add the temperature, the measured specific gravity and the reading conditions. Also record interventions and the volume removed for analysis. Memory easily forgets a small sample, especially when it seems tiny.

A regular external check can be brief while remaining attentive. Adapt measurements to the observed development and to your fermentation protocol. You are seeking a coherent story, made of several consistent clues. If the clues contradict one another, the next chapter will help you ask the right questions.

The juice does not need a permanent audience to keep fermenting. It needs suitable conditions and thoughtful monitoring. Between visits, leave the notebook closed and the gas device working. Learning to observe also means knowing when to take your hands away.
