# Finding Words for a Glass

You bring the glass closer, and something reminds you of a cut apple. Or a pear, or flowers, or simply a memory difficult to name. Describing cider often begins with that hesitation. Your nose does not have to speak like an encyclopedia.

Tasting can remain an attentive conversation. You observe, compare, then seek an accurate formulation. French offers you useful nuances for sharing these sensations. Saying “this suggests to me” leaves more room than declaring “this contains”.

Let us begin with appearance in a clean glass and under comparable light. The colour may recall straw, gold or amber. You can specify that it seems pale, deep or slightly coppery. These words describe what you see, without identifying a process on their own.

Then observe clarity and any bubbles. A slight haze differs from dense cloudiness. But remember that appearance does not certify safety. Here we are tasting a sound product, not a suspect batch to examine with the mouth.

Smell once without swirling the glass vigorously. Then swirl it gently if you wish to compare impressions. An overfilled glass makes this action more athletic than necessary. The tablecloth generally prefers a quiet tasting.

First seek an aroma family without collecting names. Does the nose seem fruity, floral or more marked by fermentation? Only then specify what seems recognisable to you. “A note of ripe apple” can be a sufficiently precise description.

A pear aroma does not mean that pears have been added. Fermentation can produce compounds suggesting different fruits. Likewise, a suggestion of honey does not prove a honey addition. A sensory comparison is an image, not an ingredient list.

Then take a small amount to observe sensations in the mouth. Tastes, aromas and texture meet. Aromas perceived through the retronasal route contribute to that overall impression. In other words, your nose continues working during tasting.

Let us revisit three distinctions already encountered beside the first apples. Acidity brings a sour or sharp sensation. Bitterness is another taste that you can describe separately. Astringency concerns a sensation of dryness or tightening in the mouth.

These impressions can overlap without becoming synonyms. A cider can be only slightly bitter yet quite astringent. It can also suggest a sweet fruit while remaining dry. Separating the words helps you clarify your preference.

For texture, ask yourself how the cider fills the mouth. Does it seem light, full or a little thin? Does a sensation of warmth seem subtle or dominant to you? These descriptions remain perceptions, not alcohol analyses.

After spitting out or swallowing the small sip, observe what persists. This is often called the finish or persistence. A fruity note may remain and then give way to slight bitterness. Describe that movement without organising a competition of stopwatches.

To sum up a glass, three clear sentences may suffice. You might write that a fruity nose recalls ripe apple. Then that lively acidity balances a fairly full palate. Finally, that slight bitterness persists after the sip.

This portrait is an example of phrasing, not the predicted result of our batch. To express a preference, add what pleases or bothers you. “I prefer it this way” does not mean the same as “it is better for everyone”. An explained preference is more useful than a verdict without a reason.

At the table, pairings become another comparison. You might try a lively cider with a bite of creamy cheese. Or compare a plain galette with the same galette accompanied by mushrooms. These are possibilities to explore, not marriages for life.

Record how the bite changes your perception of the next glass. Keep water nearby, take breaks and stop when attention fades. Your vocabulary will grow with comparisons without requiring greater consumption. It will accompany you back to the orchard where the next question is already beginning.
