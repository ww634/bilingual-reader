# The Art of Small Blends

Two ciders can tell two different stories. One seems lively, the other brings a fuller sensation. Bringing them together can create a new balance. But before mixing the vessels, let us begin with a few small glasses.

In chapter nine, we were blending juices before fermentation. This time, we are comparing ciders already fermented. The question changes because their flavours have already developed. We are assessing their meeting without claiming to return to the starting juice.

Our first batch remains the one from the previous journey. It is still intended to become dry and still. To explain the trials, let us introduce two other ciders that are entirely imaginary. They are not reserves that have suddenly appeared behind our ten-litre vessel.

Let us call these ciders A and B, with clearly legible labels. Let us suppose they are sound and come from completed fermentations. For this exercise, they are dry and no sweetening ingredient is added. These assumptions help explain the method rather than certify actual products.

Taste each component separately under comparable conditions. Record what dominates and what is missing to your taste. A might have marked acidity, B a more pronounced tannic structure. These are possible portraits, not properties attached to letters of the alphabet.

Prepare identical glasses, a graduated instrument and a sheet of notes. Identify each trial before pouring because memories quickly start looking alike. Keep a sample of each cider on its own as a point of comparison. A control helps you avoid preferring the last glass simply because it is new.

Let us choose a volume of fifty millilitres for each trial blend. The first contains forty millilitres of A and ten of B. The second contains thirty millilitres of A and twenty of B. The third combines twenty-five millilitres of each cider, that is, equal parts.

Mix gently, then compare the trials at the same temperature. You may be seeking less dominant acidity without losing aromatic freshness. Or you may prefer a softer sensation without erasing all definition. A good blend answers an intention, not an obligation to combine everything.

Taste small amounts, take breaks and keep water nearby. You can spit out the samples during this comparison. To limit expectations, ask someone to code the glasses without revealing the proportions. Your palate does not need to know the winner before the contest.

A blend can balance different qualities. It must not be used to conceal a suspect batch. A small problematic quantity can compromise a great deal of good cider. Faults do not know the manners of discreet guests.

If you like a trial, recreate it with fresh measurements. Check that the preference appears again rather than chasing a fleeting impression. Another session can also help to confirm your choice. The aim is a reproducible decision, not a flash of genius impossible to find the next day.

To change scale, convert the volumes into proportions. Forty millilitres out of fifty represent eighty per cent. The remaining ten millilitres represent twenty per cent. The sum must account for the whole blend.

Let us imagine a separate preparation of five litres in this proportion. It would require four litres of A and one litre of B. This calculation does not change the volume of our first batch. The samples taken also remain outside the reserve vessels.

Before an actual blend, check the available volumes and the condition of each component. Then measure the relevant characteristics of the blend itself. pH cannot be calculated by a simple average of the two values. And stability must be assessed for the product that will actually be packaged.

If one component contains fermentable sugars, the reasoning becomes different. The blend may bring together those sugars and yeast capable of using them. A successful tasting does not replace control of that possibility. Then return to the distinctions in chapter fifteen before proceeding.

Finally, keep the exact recipe of your preferred trial. Add the observations, the conditions and the reason for the choice. Blending resembles a conversation in which each voice should remain understandable. It now remains to give the cider packaging worthy of this care.
