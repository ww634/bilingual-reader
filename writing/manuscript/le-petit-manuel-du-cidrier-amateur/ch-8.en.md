# Understanding Your Juice

In the glass, the juice looks simple. In the notebook, it begins to raise questions. How much sugar might it provide for fermentation? How can we describe its acidity with something more than a grimace? A few measurements will help you, provided you do not ask an instrument to know everything.

Let us begin with a sample. Gently mix the juice so that the sample represents the batch. Use clean equipment that has been sanitised, then take the amount needed into a separate container. Do not pour the handled sample back into the batch. The juice set aside for tasting stays separate from the juice that receives reagents.

The hydrometer is a floating instrument. It sinks to different depths depending on the liquid. On the scale of specific gravity, it compares the liquid's density with that of water. This specific gravity is a ratio, without units. The word relative therefore has a precise job, even if it sounds rather unassuming.

First check the reference temperature shown on the instrument or in its instructions. Some models are calibrated at twenty degrees Celsius. At that temperature, a check using the water specified in the instructions should correspond to the reference value. For a scale of specific gravity, the water reference is one point zero zero zero.

Fill the trial jar with enough juice for the instrument to float freely. It should touch neither the bottom nor the sides. Remove any attached bubbles and let the reading settle. Position your eyes at liquid level, following the instruction for reading the meniscus specific to the model. The meniscus is the small curve formed by the liquid surface against the stem.

Record the value and the temperature of the sample. If the temperature differs from the reference temperature, use the manufacturer's correction or bring the sample to the required temperature. Do not correct a number at random to make it more reassuring. An instrument does not become more accurate because you like its answer.

In a juice before fermentation, dissolved sugars strongly influence density. However, other dissolved substances contribute to the measurement. The hydrometer does not count sugar molecules one by one. It provides useful information for estimating and comparing, within the limits of the method.

Suppose that a juice reads a specific gravity of 1.050. That does not mean that it contains five per cent alcohol. Fermentation has not yet begun. A potential-alcohol scale shows an estimate based on assumptions, not the result of an alcohol analysis. Later we will use the change in density to monitor the batch.

A refractometer uses light to provide a different reading. Its scale in degrees Brix is useful for juice, but is not interchangeable with every other scale. After the start of fermentation, alcohol changes the reading. An uncorrected Brix value then does not allow you to read directly the remaining sugar.

Let us turn to acidity. pH and titratable acidity describe different aspects of the same juice. pH is related to hydrogen-ion activity. Titratable acidity measures the amount of acid neutralised under a defined procedure. Two juices may have a similar pH and different titratable acidities.

To measure pH, use a suitable meter, calibrated using the buffer solutions specified in its instructions. Also follow the instructions for cleaning and storage of the electrode. A measurement with a poorly maintained electrode may look precise because it displays two decimal places. Decimal places do not replace maintenance.

For a titratable-acidity analysis, follow a procedure suited to apple juice or request an analysis from a laboratory. The result must specify the unit and the reference acid, often malic acid for apples. A number without those details does not travel well between notebooks.

In your notebook, separate the raw reading from the corrected value. Add the instrument name and the method used for correction. This will allow you to retrace the path between observation and the result recorded. If two readings seem incompatible to you, repeat sampling and check the equipment before changing all the juice.

A number should answer a precise question. Density helps you to follow a change; pH provides information on another aspect of the medium. Neither replaces knowledge of the juice's origin or its treatment. The good habit is not collecting numbers, but knowing what each one allows you to decide.

We now have words for tasting and tools for measuring. No isolated number certifies safety or final quality of the cider. For our example, the suggested readings remain hypothetical; they explain a way of working. The next choice will be to decide what balance we want to prepare.
