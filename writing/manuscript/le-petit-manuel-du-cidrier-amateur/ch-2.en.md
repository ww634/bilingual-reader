# Learning to Taste an Apple

Put two apples on a plate. Choose sound fruit, wash them under running water and cut a quarter from each. For this small comparison, you need neither a diploma nor an impressive vocabulary. You need to slow down a little. The apple will wait; your inbox can do the same.

Before tasting, look at the flesh and smell the cut fruit. A smell may remind you of a flower, grass or a pear. These are comparisons, not a list of ingredients. If you smell simply “apple”, write it down. An honest word is better than an imaginary bouquet.

Take a small bite and chew without rushing. The sweet sensation is often easy to recognise. It can fill the first moment, then leave more room for something else. Ask yourself whether the second apple seems to you sweeter, less sweet or similar. You are comparing impressions, you are not yet measuring a quantity of sugar.

Next comes acidity. Think of the liveliness of a lemon wedge, without expecting the same intensity in every apple. A tangy apple can make your mouth water and give an impression of freshness. This freshness does not mean that the fruit is cold. The same word sometimes describes a temperature, sometimes a taste sensation.

Do not confuse sour and bitter. Bitterness is more reminiscent of that of unsweetened coffee, although the apple has its own expression. A bitter flavour can linger after the bite. It is not necessarily a fault, but its intensity matters. A deep voice can enrich a song without having to drown out all the other singers.

There is also astringency. The word is less familiar, but the sensation may be familiar to you. A black tea steeped for a long time can leave the mouth feeling dry, with an impression of roughness. Here we are describing a textural sensation, not simply a flavour. In apples, tannins contribute to astringency and to bitterness.

This difference deserves a pause. A mouth that feels dry does not prove that the fruit contains little sugar. The word dry, in a description of cider, generally refers to its low sweetness. The drying sensation of tannins is something else. There is a little vocabulary trap that your next glass will no longer be able to set for you.

Now return to the aromas. As you chew, you perceive smells that contribute to the overall impression. An apple can seem very aromatic without being the sweetest of the two. Try to distinguish “it smells lovely” from “it is sweet”. Your nose and your tongue work together, but they do not provide exactly the same information.

Between two fruits, drink a little water and wait a moment. Do not try to compare twelve varieties all at once. Two or three are enough to reveal differences. If possible, taste them under similar conditions. You are looking for a useful comparison, not a competition in which the last apple wins through exhaustion.

Write down a sentence for each fruit. For example: “The first apple is sweet and aromatic, but its flavour fades quickly.” Then: “The second makes my mouth water more and leaves a slightly dry sensation.” These imaginary observations do not rank the apples from best to worst. They suggest different roles.

A blend brings together several juices or ciders to seek a balance. One variety may bring acidity, another tannins. That does not mean that you must always blend or that a magic proportion works everywhere. It means that a pleasant eating apple and an apple useful to a cider maker do not necessarily answer the same question.

Imagine that you taste with a friend. You say “lively”, she says “aggressive”. Before discussing your preferences, try to describe what causes those words. Is it acidity, bitterness or a rough sensation that remains on the gums? You can agree on the description while having different preferences. Tasting does not require a unanimous vote.

Another detail deserves your attention: duration. A sensation can be strong and brief, or subtle but persistent. Try to record what happens at the beginning, then what remains after swallowing. You are adding a dimension to your description, without needing a complicated new word.

Finally, keep some room for uncertainty. “This reminds me of pear” is an honest sentence. “I do not yet know how to describe it” is another. The pleasure also comes from the moment when a familiar sensation finally finds its name.

The texture of the flesh also deserves a few words. Distinguish crispness of the fruit from the drying sensation that remains in the mouth. A bite can be juicy and subsequently leave a rough impression. The two observations do not contradict each other. By separating these sensations, you will better understand what you mean when you describe a fruit or a glass.

Classifications can help, but their categories must be understood in context. A technical category does not replace observation of the fruit actually available. We will return to measurements before preparing our juice. Today, you have mainly learned to replace a vague “good” with a few more accurate words. That is a lovely beginning for a conversation with a fruit.
