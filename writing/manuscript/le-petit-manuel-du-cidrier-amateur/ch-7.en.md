# Milling and Pressing

The first apple goes into the mill, and the orchard changes its voice. The sound of leaves gives way to the sound of work. A smell of fresh fruit rises from the pulp. This moment is very satisfying, but it is better to have read the instructions before admiring it.

This chapter follows the whole-apple route. Our first batch remains the one made from purchased juice, defined in the previous chapter. You can therefore understand pressing without having to buy a press for this attempt. If you work with your fruit, the same principles will help you to organise the day.

Just before milling, sort the apples again. A sound fruit at harvest may have deteriorated during the wait. Discard rotten fruit or mouldy fruit, then wash the selected apples with drinking-quality water. Washing removes dirt; it does not sterilise the fruit. Once milled, a bad apple becomes much harder to remove.

Prepare the equipment before loading the fruit. The mill must be stable, with its guards in place. Keep your hands out of the milling area. Use only the designated pusher if the machine requires one. Before clearing a machine, stop it and unplug it according to its instructions.

Why mill before pressing? A whole apple holds its juice within its structure. Milling breaks up the tissues and prepares a pulp whose juice will be able to flow out under pressure. The press extracts that juice; it does not replace appropriate milling. Crushing harder is not always the answer to poor preparation.

The desired texture depends on the mill and the pressing system. Pieces that are too large may leave a lot of juice in the material. A very fine purée can also make drainage difficult in some devices. Aim for the recommended texture for your equipment, by observing the actual result.

Prepare an amount that you can press without a long wait. Juice and pulp are exposed to air as soon as the fruit is opened. The colour may turn brown through oxidation. This change does not measure quality or juice safety on its own. Here we follow a route without extended maceration, that is, without deliberately resting the pulp.

Fit the cloths or pressing bags specified by the manufacturer. They must be clean and suitable for contact with juice. Distribute the pulp according to the method for your press, without exceeding its capacity. The collection vessel waits beneath the outlet, rather than at the other end of the garden.

Apply pressure gradually, following the machine's limits. Give the juice time to find its way out. On a screw press, a long improvised extension is not an improvement on the instructions. Do not exceed the permitted force to obtain a few extra drops. The aim is steady work, not a muscle contest.

The solid residue is called pomace. Its presence reminds you that the weight of apples and the volume of juice are two different things. Record the weight of prepared fruit and the volume collected if you want to compare your trials. A yield only has meaning if we know how it was calculated.

Suppose, for an imaginary calculation, that ten kilograms of apples produce six litres of juice. The volumetric yield is then six tenths of a litre per kilogram. This number illustrates a calculation, not a promise for your press. The fruit, their condition and the equipment can change the result.

The flowing juice may contain small particles. Coarse straining with suitable equipment removes unwanted fragments, but does not sanitise the liquid. Freshly pressed juice is not ready to drink simply because it smells lovely. Its food-safety preparation requires a verified method suited to the intended use.

Do not leave fresh juice waiting all day in a warm corner. Plan how it will be handled before pressing, with appropriate storage and a validated treatment where necessary. For flavour comparisons, we will use pasteurised juices. Once the work is finished, remove the pomace and clean without letting the residues on the equipment dry.

If you press with other people, divide up the roles before beginning. One person can prepare the fruit, another monitor collection. Keep one person responsible for each machine, with clear instructions. The moment when everyone believes that someone else is watching the bucket is rarely the best moment of the day.

To compare two pressing sessions, also record the working time and the difficulties encountered. A slightly higher yield is not your only criterion. You may seek better drainage, less handling or easier cleaning. A good method must also remain workable when you are tired.

The press has done its part. Your notebook now contains something more than the memory of a sticky day. It remains to understand what the juice can teach us, before asking yeast to transform it.
