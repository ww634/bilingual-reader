# Bottling at the Right Time

The bottles lined up already give the impression that everything is finished. They shine, they wait, they seem almost impatient. But packaging does not finish a fermentation for you. Before filling, you must know what you are enclosing.

Our main journey still aims for a dry, still cider. We add neither sugar for carbonation nor juice to make it sweeter. This decision simplifies the project without removing the checks. The bottling date will depend on the checks, not the calendar.

Begin by rereading the fermentation history in the notebook. Do the measurements show coherent progress towards the expected result? Has there been an unexplained slowdown, an addition or a temperature change? A suspect stop must be understood before closing bottles.

Take reliable measurements again of specific gravity with the precautions already studied. Compare several readings over a period suited to the yeast and batch conditions. The measurements must have been taken under conditions that allow us to interpret the yeast's actual activity. A spell in the cold can conceal a fermentation that has merely slowed.

Stable specific gravity is one part of the evidence, not all the evidence. Specific gravity does not directly give the quantity of remaining fermentable sugars. Alcohol and other constituents also contribute to the reading. Even if the cider seems dry, its taste is no substitute for checking its composition.

If fermentation completion remains uncertain, have the measurements assessed by someone competent in cider making. A laboratory can measure residual sugars and suggest an appropriate microbiological check. Distinguish completed alcoholic fermentation from demonstrated overall stability. It may also be necessary to check whether bacterial transformations remain possible.

Bottle only when fermentation completion and the intended stability are established for this product. While significant doubt remains, continue checking and maintain appropriate storage conditions, without bottling. A suspect smell or possible contamination must also be assessed before bottling. The bottle is not a cupboard for putting away difficult questions.

Once these conditions are met, organise the filling station. Separate dirty equipment from prepared equipment with a clear route for the bottles. Prepare enough containers for the volume actually available with a small margin. Our initial ten litres still do not constitute a promise of final yield.

Choose bottles designed for the drink and the intended conditions. Check their compatibility with the closures and the fitting tool. A vessel intended for still cider must not be used for fermentation under pressure. A strong bottle does not make uncontrolled fermentation acceptable.

Inspect the neck, the sides and the base of every bottle. Set aside any that are damaged, chipped or difficult to clean properly. Light helps reveal residues and some visible defects. It does not measure the mechanical strength of the glass.

Clean, then sanitise according to the chosen product and equipment. Follow the specified doses, contact time and any required rinsing. Also prepare the closures and accessories according to their own instructions. Then avoid touching the surfaces that will contact the cider.

Transfer gently with clean, sanitised equipment. An appropriate filling device can help limit splashing and losses. Keep the outlet near the bottom and then beneath the liquid when the device allows it. Follow its instructions to obtain consistent operation.

The filling level must suit the bottle and closure. Leave the space specified in their guidance without filling to overflowing. A large air space increases exposure to oxygen. But eliminating all space is not a universal rule for good filling.

Close each bottle with the appropriate method and tool. Check the appearance of the closure without improvising a pressure test. An incorrectly fitted closure requires correction according to the equipment instructions. Your arm does not replace a machine setting.

Identify the batch and date on the bottles or their storage place. Record the packaged volume, the number of bottles and the observed losses. Keep the results on which the bottling decision was based. A legible label is a gift to your future memory.

Finally, plan storage checks suited to the process. Bottling completes a stage, but monitoring the product continues. If unexpected pressure or an abnormality appears, stop serving the batch and seek advice. Once the cider has been properly packaged, let us turn to storage and serving.
