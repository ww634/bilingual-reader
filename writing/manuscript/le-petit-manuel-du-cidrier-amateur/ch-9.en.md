# Choosing a Balance

On the table, three small glasses are waiting for your judgement. One contains a juice that you find very lively. Another seems rounder. The third combines some of each. You have just created an interesting question, with less equipment than it takes for a picnic.

A blend is not an automatic way to improve everything that goes into the glass. It serves to seek a particular balance using raw materials that you understand. Before blending, say what you want to change. “Less acidic on the palate” is an objective. “More professional” is mainly a vague expression.

Remember the sensations from the second chapter. Sweetness, acidity, bitterness and astringency do not replace one another. A less acidic juice does not necessarily bring the tannins that you are missing. A very fragrant aroma does not guarantee a balanced palate. Consider each quality before adding up your impressions.

For a first trial, choose two pasteurised juices whose composition you know. Give them simple names, such as A and B. Do not change their serving temperature halfway through the comparison. Prepare clean containers and a measuring tool that is precise enough for small volumes. The tasting trials stay separate from the batch to be fermented.

Begin by tasting A on its own, then B on its own. Record their qualities and their limitations in precise words. You can then prepare three mixtures of the same volume. For example, in a hundred millilitres, try seventy-five millilitres of A and twenty-five of B. Compare with fifty of each, then with twenty-five of A and seventy-five of B.

This small set allows you to perceive a direction. You do not have to choose a final proportion immediately. If the half-and-half mixture appeals to you, you can refine the comparison around that point. Work with recorded quantities, not just with the memory of a flick of the wrist.

It is tempting to give the preferred mixture a flattering label. “The best” sounds good, but closes the discussion rather quickly. Instead write why you prefer it today. This will allow you to compare your choice with another person's choice without debating the word best for an hour.

To move from a small trial to a larger volume, keep the proportions. Seventy-five millilitres of A out of a hundred represent three quarters of the mixture. For ten litres, that gives seven and a half litres of A and two and a half litres of B. This calculation illustrates a change of scale, not a change to our main batch.

Our main batch remains the purchased juice chosen in chapter six. Juices A and B are separate teaching examples. This separation prevents turning our notebook into a detective investigation. If you decide to make your own blend, record its origin, its proportions and its actual volume.

pH cannot be calculated using a simple average of the values for your two juices. Measure the mixture actually obtained. The taste impression also does not always follow a linear calculation. Sweetness can mask some of the perceived acidity, without making it disappear. Your measurements and your sensations complement each other.

Above all, you are tasting a juice, not yet the future cider. When yeast consume much of the sugar, the perceived balance will change. A mixture that is delicious today does not constitute a guarantee for tomorrow. The comparison prepares a considered choice; it does not remove the learning that will follow.

Do not add sugar simply to make the juice more pleasant to drink. That additional sugar may be fermented and increase potential alcohol. Likewise, an acidity adjustment or a tannin addition requires a method, a suitable product and a reason supported by measurements. A small glass should not become an improvised laboratory without a procedure.

Trials can also teach you to change nothing. If your juice suits your goal, you do not have to invent a problem with it just to use all your accessories. Keep the notes from rejected trials as well as those from the favourite. They will save you having to repeat the same discovery at every harvest.

To check your preference, you can repeat a small comparison using codes that hide the proportions. Ask someone to change the order of the glasses. You may discover that the mixture you expected most from is not your favourite. That is interesting information, not a defeat.

If you prepare an actual blend, allow for the volume needed for sampling. A hundred millilitres used for tasting do not return to the vessel because your calculation requires a neat round total. Measure what actually remains before calculating later additions. Precision is above all a matter of consistency between your actions and your notes.

We now know what we want to observe and preserve. The juice has an identity, the choices have a reason, and the notebook remains understandable. It is time to meet the organisms that will transform this preparation into cider.
