# Preparing a Small Workspace

The crates are ready, the apples are identified, and your enthusiasm already fills half the room. Leave a little room for the equipment. Before producing a single drop, we will organise the actions and the objects that will make the work possible. A good workspace spares you, above all, having to look for something with sticky hands.

Let us begin with a practical distinction. You can start with your fruit, or choose a purchased juice. The first route requires preparing the apples, milling them and pressing them. The second allows you to focus your first attempt on fermentation. These are two starting points into the same craft.

For our imaginary batch, let us now establish this choice. We will use ten litres of purchased juice, pasteurised and without preservatives that inhibit yeast. The orchard apples will serve us in understanding the other route, without being added to this first batch. This will allow us to follow a precise example while learning how to work with fruit.

Read the ingredients list rather than the photograph of the orchard on the packaging. Sorbate and benzoate are among the preservatives to avoid for this starting method. Pasteurised does not mean impossible to ferment. However, an opened bottle is no longer protected by its sealed packaging. Follow the storage conditions specified by the supplier until it is used.

Organise the space by following the route of the product. The still-dirty crates stay away from the accessories prepared for the juice. Provide drinking-quality water, a washable surface and a place to put the clean utensils. A clean hose placed on the floor has just changed category.

The fermentation vessel must be suitable for food contact and for this use. Choose a model that is easy to clean, with a suitable lid and a device that allows gas to escape. The airlock is one such device. It allows the escape of carbon dioxide through a liquid, according to its design. Follow its instructions for assembly and filling.

Ten litres of juice do not mean a vessel filled to the brim. You need to leave the space specified for fermentation by the manufacturer. Check the recommended working volume, not just the advertised volume. Foam does not consult the scale before rising.

For measuring, provide a thermometer, a hydrometer and a trial jar of the right size. A small graduated container will be useful for trials. For transferring, use a clean device suited to drinks, for example a mechanically primed siphon. Do not prime a hose with your mouth.

Cleaning and sanitising are two different operations. Cleaning removes visible residues and dirt. Sanitising equipment reduces unwanted microorganisms on an already clean surface. It does not replace cleaning and does not make your workspace sterile.

Take apart the parts that require it, clean with a compatible product, then rinse as directed. Then apply a sanitiser intended for food equipment. Dilution, contact time and any rinsing depend on the product used. “No-rinse” does not mean “no measuring”. Never mix cleaning products to make them more effective.

Taps, seals and the inside of hoses deserve as much attention as the large vessel. Replace a cracked part or one that is too scratched to be cleaned properly. Also prepare a clean place to store the equipment after use. The next pressing day begins at the end of this one.

Before buying, look at what you already own, then check its intended use. A bucket that looks clean is not necessarily intended to hold a drink. Avoid containers whose history or material are unknown. Food-grade plastic and suitable stainless steel are common options, but check each accessory. Compatibility also concerns the products used for cleaning that material.

Make an assembly trial before the big day. Check that the tap closes, that the fittings hold and that the hose reaches the next vessel. Using water, you can spot a leak without losing juice. After this trial, repeat cleaning and sanitising before any contact with the cider.

Finally think about your hands. Wash them before handling clean equipment, and again after a dirty task. Take the prepared accessories by the parts that will not touch the juice. These small actions preserve the cleaning work instead of undoing it.

Before starting, mentally follow the route of the juice. Where will it be collected? How will it be measured? Where will the sample go? Prepare the labels and the notebook before needing an extra hand. We now have a workspace, a hygiene method and a clear route. The apples can come in: the mill is waiting for them.
